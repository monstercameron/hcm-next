# US Federal Employment Law Baseline

**State:** US federal | **Researched:** 2026-09-03 | **Status:** DRAFTED

## 1. Summary for Human Capital Management Suite

**Wage and overtime rules:**

- FLSA minimum wage $7.25/hour (unchanged since 2009), overtime required at 1.5x after 40 hours/week for all non-exempt employees; no state may pay less than federal minimum.
- EAP (executive/administrative/professional) exemption salary threshold $35,568/year ($684/week) after November 2024 rule vacatur; verify any future rulemaking.
- Youth minimum wage $4.25/hour for employees under 20 during first 90 days; tip credit up to $5.15/hour.

**Leave and notice obligations:**

- FMLA: 12 weeks/year unpaid leave, 1,250 hours in 12 months, for employers 50+ within 75 miles; 26 weeks military caregiver leave.
- PUMP Act: unpaid break time and private space for nursing mothers during first year after childbirth.
- WARN Act (60 days' notice) applies to employers 100+ employees; triggered by plant closing 50+ or mass layoff 50+ employees or 33%+ of workforce.

**Background checks and verification:**

- FCRA (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.): written authorization, pre-adverse and adverse action notice required before hiring/promotion decisions; applies to all background checks.
- E-Verify: mandatory only for federal contractors (FAR clause); voluntary for private sector unless state requires.

**EEO and antidiscrimination statutes:**

- Title VII (15+ employees), ADEA (20+ employees), ADA (15+ employees), EPA (any size), PDA (15+ employees), PWFA (15+ employees), GINA (15+ employees).
- EEOC charge deadline: 180 days from discriminatory act (or 300 days in states with fair-employment agency deferral agreements).
- NLRA § 7: protects union activity, concerted activity, and collective bargaining; applies to all employers.

**Antiretaliation and job security:**

- USERRA: prohibits discrimination based on military service; requires reemployment with accrued seniority.
- NLRB § 7: retaliation for union/concerted activity is an unfair labor practice.
- Wrongful-discharge exception: at-will employment does not permit termination for illegal reasons.

**Record-keeping:**

- FLSA: retain payroll 3 years, supplementary time records 2 years.
- OSHA: employers 11+ must record injuries on Form 300; retain 5 years.
- I-9: retain 3 years after hire or 1 year after termination, whichever is later.

**Thresholds:** COBRA (20+ employees), ERISA (varies by plan type), EO 11246 (federal contractors only; EO rescinded January 2025, grace period ended April 2025).

## 2. Employment Relationship

**At-will employment:** No federal statute mandates or creates employment at-will; common law controls in most states. At-will status means either the employer or employee may terminate the relationship at any time, with or without cause, unless a contract, statute, or public policy provides otherwise. Exceptions exist: NLRA § 7 (union activity and concerted protected activities), USERRA (military service reemployment rights), WARN Act (60-day notice for mass separations), and state statutory exceptions (non-compete covenants, wrongful-discharge statutes, implied contracts from handbooks, etc.). The burden of proof is on the employee to plead and prove an exception.

**At-will limitations:** An at-will relationship does not permit termination for an illegal reason (retaliation for protected activity, discrimination on a protected basis, refusal to commit an unlawful act). Termination for cause still requires that the cause be legal; employers cannot terminate for discriminatory reasons, NLRA retaliation, OSHA complaints, military service, bankruptcy discharge, jury duty, or whistleblowing.

**Written employment agreements and offer letters:** No federal requirement mandates written agreements. However, state laws and the FCRA impose disclosure duties. For example, the FCRA requires written disclosure that a background check will be used before obtaining authorization. Written offer letters should reference the key terms (title, pay, start date, reporting relationship, at-will status where applicable, any conditions such as background clearance). Written agreements are valuable for establishing the actual terms of employment and avoiding misunderstanding claims.

**NLRA § 7 protections:** Section 7 of the National Labor Relations Act protects employees' rights to form, join, or assist labor unions, to bargain collectively, and to engage in other concerted activities for mutual aid and protection (including strikes, walkouts, complaints about wages, hours, or working conditions). Employers may not interfere with, restrain, or coerce employees in the exercise of these rights (section 8(a)(1)). Retaliation for NLRA activity is an unfair labor practice and can result in backpay, reinstatement, and NLRB orders to cease and desist.

## 3. Wages

**FLSA minimum wage:** $7.25/hour (29 U.S.C. § 206), unchanged since July 24, 2009. This minimum applies to all non-exempt employees engaged in interstate commerce or covered by the FLSA's enterprise coverage rule. Employers may not pay less and may not ask an employee to work below minimum wage. Tip credit permitted up to $5.15/hour (29 C.F.R. § 531.20); employer may claim $2.13 direct wage if tips bring total to $7.25/hour minimum. The tip credit rule requires an employer to pay the $2.13 "tipped minimum" directly and ensure tips supplement it to the full $7.25/hour.

**Overtime:** Required at 1.5x regular rate (time-and-a-half) for all hours over 40 in a workweek (29 U.S.C. § 207). No daily or daily-threshold limit; only the workweek 40-hour rule applies federally. Employers subject to FLSA must compute and pay overtime; no exemption exists except for certain statutory categories (executive, administrative, professional, computer, outside sales, and highly compensated employees meeting the salary and duty thresholds).

**Youth rates:** Minimum wage $4.25/hour for employees under 20 during their first 90 calendar days of employment with that employer; the federal youth opportunity wage (29 C.F.R. § 516.23). After 90 days or upon reaching age 20, whichever comes first, the $7.25 minimum applies.

**EAP exemption salary (2024–2026):** $35,568/year ($684/week). On November 15, 2024, the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Texas vacated DOL's April 26, 2024 final rule, which had raised thresholds to $844/week effective July 1, 2024, and $1,128/week effective January 1, 2025. The $684 threshold (last adopted in 2004) remains operative. Future judicial or legislative action may change this. Verify current status before applying to any individual transaction.

**Three-part EAP test:** Salary threshold alone does not exempt a role. An employer must also verify that the employee's duties satisfy the "primary duty" test: executive (supervisory authority), administrative (discretion in matters of significance), or professional (specialized knowledge, advanced training, predominant intellectual work). Misclassification liability attaches if any prong fails.

**Record retention (29 C.F.R. part 516):** Employers must retain payroll records 3 years, supplementary time and earnings records 2 years. Records must include employee name, address, date of birth, sex, occupation, rate/basis of pay, hours worked each workday and each workweek, overtime pay computation, and deductions. Payroll data is discoverable in FLSA litigation and FLSA audits.

No federal mandate on pay frequency; state law controls. Most states require payment at least semi-monthly or monthly. No federal final-pay timing rule on separation; state law controls whether final pay is due same-day, next-payday, or within N days of termination.

## 4. Pay Transparency and Equity

No federal requirement to disclose salary ranges in job postings or on request at the federal level. Some states and localities impose such requirements; verify state and local law. The Equal Pay Act (29 U.S.C. § 206(d)) requires equal pay for substantially equal work regardless of sex; applies to employers of any size with no minimum-employee threshold. The "substantially equal" test compares work performed, not job titles or classifications. Liquidated damages (double back pay) available for willful violations; 2-year statute of limitations (3 years for willful). The EPA does not require an EEOC charge before lawsuit.

Title VII § 703(h) (42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(h)) permits compensation practices that reflect factors other than sex (seniority, merit pay, production systems, piece rates) without liability, provided they were not motivated by sex. A pay disparity that correlates with a protected class may trigger scrutiny.

**Equal Pay Act vs. Title VII:** The Equal Pay Act covers all compensation disparity on the basis of sex; Title VII covers sex discrimination in any term of employment including compensation. Both apply concurrently; Title VII requires 15+ employees, EPA does not.

## 5. Leave and Time

**FMLA (29 U.S.C. § 2601):** Employers with 50 or more employees within a 75-mile radius must offer 12 weeks (480 hours) of **unpaid** leave per 12-month period for qualifying reasons: childbirth/adoption, employee's own serious health condition, care for a family member's serious health condition, military service, military caregiver leave (for a covered servicemember with a serious illness/injury), or qualifying exigency leave (related to a spouse/child/parent's active-duty military service). Eligibility requires: (1) 12 months of employment with the employer; (2) 1,250 hours of service in the preceding 12 months; and (3) employment at a worksite where the employer has 50+ employees within 75 miles. Military caregiver leave entitles the employee to 26 workweeks in a single 12-month period (not combined with the 12-week standard entitlement; it replaces the 12-week standard). Employers must maintain the employee's health insurance during FMLA leave as if the employee were actively working. Upon return, the employee must be restored to the same or an equivalent job.

**12-month period calculation:** Employers may use a calendar year, rolling 12-month period, fiscal year, or "workweek" rolling calculation. The method must be selected before the first FMLA leave and must be applied consistently.

**Substitution of paid leave:** Employers may require that an employee use accrued paid leave (vacation, sick leave, PTO) as part of the 12 weeks of FMLA leave, unless state law forbids it. The employee's right to paid leave is not expanded by FMLA; FMLA requires only that the 12 weeks be available (paid or unpaid, depending on the employer's policy).

**PUMP Act (Lactation Accommodation):** Employers must provide unpaid reasonable break time and an appropriate private place (other than a bathroom) for employees to express breast milk during the first year after a child's birth (29 U.S.C. § 207(r), as amended December 2025). The break time should be paid, but if breaks are unpaid, the employee's time expressing milk counts as work time that must be paid. Employers with fewer than 50 employees at a worksite where the employee works may be exempt if the requirement causes an undue hardship.

**No federal paid sick leave mandate.** Paid sick leave, personal days, or paid time off (PTO) are entirely discretionary at the federal level and vary by state and employer policy. Some states require paid sick leave; federal law does not.

## 6. Records and Access

**FLSA record retention (29 C.F.R. part 516):**

- Payroll records (including name, address, date of birth, sex, occupation, rate/basis of pay, hours per workday and workweek, overtime computations, deductions) 3 years minimum.
- Supplementary records (time cards, work schedules, wage-rate tables for piecework, overtime-rate calculations) 2 years minimum.
- Specific wage and hour facts (basis for overtime rate, overtime pay calculations, tip credit reconciliations) 3 years.
- Records are discoverable in FLSA litigation and inspected during DOL Wage and Hour Division audits.

**OSHA (29 C.F.R. part 1904):** Employers with 11+ employees must record occupational injuries and illnesses on OSHA 300 (Log of Work-Related Injuries and Illnesses), 300-A (Summary), and 301 (Incident Report) forms; retain 5 years following the end of the year. Electronic storage permitted. Failure to record results in penalties up to $16,131 per violation (2024 inflation-adjusted penalty).

**I-9 retention (8 C.F.R. § 274a.2):** Employers must retain Form I-9 for each employee for 3 years after hire OR 1 year after termination, whichever is later. I-9 storage may be electronic. No update or re-verification is required when an employee's pay or role changes, even if the change is substantial.

No federal statute grants employees a right to inspect their own personnel files. The FCRA gives individuals a right to request and receive a free copy of their credit report if adverse action was taken based on it, but this is a consumer-report right, not a general personnel-file-access right. State law may grant access; verify state requirements.

## 7. Privacy and Data

**HIPAA (45 C.F.R. parts 160, 164):** HIPAA is often misunderstood to apply to all employers. In fact, HIPAA applies only to health plans, health care providers, and health care clearinghouses. An employer _sponsoring_ a health plan is not automatically a HIPAA covered entity unless it also processes health plan claims or enrollment data directly (as opposed to having a third-party administrator do so). Employment records maintained by HR (sick notes, fitness-for-duty letters, accommodation requests, medical inquiries) are explicitly excluded from HIPAA's definition of Protected Health Information (PHI) and are **not** covered by HIPAA, even if the employer sponsors a health plan.

**ADA medical information:** Medical information collected during interactive process or accommodation request is subject to the ADA's strict confidentiality requirements but not HIPAA. The ADA requires medical records to be kept in separate, confidential files.

**State privacy laws (CCPA, CPRA, BIPA, etc.):** May extend to employee personal data if they state so explicitly. California's CPRA, for example, explicitly covers employee data; Illinois' BIPA covers employee biometric information. Verify applicable state law before processing employee biometric data or personal information.

**Breach notification:** No federal statute mandates breach notification for general employee personal data. State breach-notification laws typically apply when an unauthorized disclosure of "personal information" (names, SSNs, financial accounts, health data) occurs. The standards, notice timing (without unreasonable delay, typically 30–60 days), and credit-monitoring requirements vary by state. Verify state law.

## 8. Hiring and Background

**FCRA (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.):** Applies whenever an employer (or a third-party consumer reporting agency on the employer's behalf) obtains a background check, credit report, or any report containing information on a person's character, reputation, or personal characteristics. The FCRA mandates: (1) **written disclosure** that a background check will be used for employment purposes, provided to the candidate before the check is ordered; (2) **written authorization** for the check; (3) **pre-adverse action notice** (copy of the report + FTC's "Summary of Your Rights Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act") to the candidate before a final adverse hiring/promotion/retention decision is made; and (4) **adverse action notice** (CRA name/address/phone, statement that the CRA did not make the hiring decision, notice of right to dispute accuracy, notice of right to request a free re-report within 60 days) after the adverse decision is final. Non-compliance carries civil penalties up to $2,568 per violation (2024 inflation-adjusted), plus statutory damages in private litigation.

**Scope:** The FCRA applies to checks performed in connection with employment (hiring, promotion, retention, demotion, termination) for any size employer. "Employment purposes" includes evaluation for initial hire, promotion, reassignment, retention, and separation. A background check ordered after an internal promotion counts; prior clearance at hire does not satisfy the obligation for a new promotion-contingent check.

**No federal ban-the-box law.** Twenty-one state files mention ban-the-box restrictions (timing, conditional questions, etc.); federal baseline imposes no restrictions on the timing of inquiries or criminal-history questions. The timing and permissibility of criminal inquiries vary by state.

**E-Verify (IRCA verification):** Voluntary for private-sector employers unless required by state law (some states mandate it for certain industries or all public-facing employees). Mandatory for federal contractors and subcontractors with a FAR E-Verify clause in their contract; contractors must enroll in E-Verify within 30 days of contract award and verify new hires within 90 days of enrollment. Non-compliance may result in contract termination, debarment, or civil penalties.

**No federal drug-testing standard;** state law controls permissibility, notice requirements, and remedies for false positives. Some states require notice before testing; others permit surprise tests. The ADA restricts drug testing to post-offer and may not test for lawful medical use of prescription drugs.

## 9. Separation

**WARN Act (29 U.S.C. § 2101):** The Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act applies to employers with 100 or more employees (counted as full-time employees, or employees working a combined 4,000+ hours per week). Requires 60 calendar days' advance written notice to affected employees and to state/local authorities before a **plant closing** (affecting 50+ employees) or a **mass layoff** (affecting 50+ employees OR 33%+ of the workforce at a site, or 500+ employees regardless of percentage, in any 30-day period). Notice must be given to the employee, the state dislocated-worker office, and the chief elected official of the local government. Penalties for non-compliance: up to 60 days' back pay and benefits per affected employee (including health insurance continuation), plus civil penalties up to $500 per employee or $30,000 total. Affected employees may sue in federal court; liability is joint and several.

**Exceptions:** WARN does not apply to temporary layoffs (under 6 months), strikes, replacement of unfair-labor-practice strikers, or separations where the business is relocated but the employee is offered a transfer with no material change in compensation within 90 days.

**No federal mini-WARN for smaller separations.** State mini-WARN laws (applying to smaller reductions, shorter notice periods, or other thresholds) apply independently and do not preempt federal WARN. Both may apply concurrently; the stricter rule governs.

**No federal severance requirement.** Severance is purely discretionary unless required by state law, a contract, a CBA, or an employer handbook.

**No federal notice of termination requirement** at the separation moment. State law varies widely: some states require no notice, others require notice on the final day, others require notice before termination (rare). Final-pay timing is controlled by state law; most states mandate payment within a specified period (typically 2 weeks to 1 month after separation). The FLSA does not govern final-pay timing.

## 10. Classification and Multi-State

**Independent contractor classification (FLSA, 29 U.S.C. § 203):** Federal law treats a worker as an employee (and therefore covered by FLSA minimum wage and overtime) unless the worker qualifies as an independent contractor. The test is an "economic reality" multifactor analysis: degree of control, opportunity for profit/loss, investment in tools/premises, permanence of relationship, skill required, integration into the business, and any written agreement. No single factor is dispositive. As of May 2025, DOL reversed enforcement of the Biden 2024 rule and returned to pre-2024 guidance, emphasizing the "common law" economic-reality test. The ABC test proposed by labor advocates was rejected by DOL and does not bind federal FLSA enforcement. States may adopt their own tests (ABC, Borello, etc.); federal FLSA classification does not determine state classification. No minimum-employee threshold applies; all employers and all contractors are covered.

**Verify current DOL rulemaking:** The February 2026 DOL proposed rule (comment deadline April 28, 2026) may rescind the 2024 rule and reinstate 2021-era principles. Proposed rules do not have legal effect until finalized. Employers should monitor publication in the Federal Register.

**Remote work and multi-state exposure:** No federal rule determines which state's law applies when an employee works remotely from a state other than the employer's principal place of business or the employee's home state. Common law conflict-of-laws principles may invoke the situs of work, the situs of injury, the employer's principal place of business, or the employee's residence. The result is unpredictable. Most employers apply the law of the state where the employee performs work. However, FLSA, WARN Act, and OSHA apply federally to all qualifying employers and employees regardless of state line. Employers must track and model all applicable state law; exposure is often multi-state even for a single employee.

## 11. Implications for P1A/P1B: Promotion and Base-Pay Change

A promotion and base-pay change in Human Capital Management Suite must trigger evaluation of the following federal obligations:

**1. FLSA reclassification:** If the base-pay change crosses the EAP threshold ($35,568/year, currently), re-verify whether the _duties_ qualify for exemption. The three-part test requires: (a) salary at or above the threshold; (b) primary duty as executive (supervisory), administrative (discretionary), or professional (specialized knowledge, advanced training); and (c) discretionary exercise of judgment in matters of significance. Reclassifying from non-exempt to exempt requires an affirmative determination that duties meet all three prongs. Reclassifying from exempt to non-exempt requires documenting that the duties no longer meet the test. Updated payroll records must document the change date, new pay rate, new classification, and overtime calculation method going forward (if changing to non-exempt). No waiting period between the decision and implementation. Misclassification carries back-wage liability and liquidated damages.

**2. Overtime record-keeping updates:** If changing from exempt to non-exempt, begin recording hours worked each day and each week and calculating overtime at 1.5x after 40/week. If changing from non-exempt to exempt, cease hour recording and shift to salary-only compensation. Retain the old time records per 29 C.F.R. § 516.6 (2 years minimum).

**3. FCRA compliance (if background check gates promotion):** If the promotion is contingent on a new background check (even a re-check with the same CRA), you must comply with the full FCRA sequence: (a) write disclosure that a check will be used for employment purposes; (b) obtain written authorization; (c) order the check; (d) provide pre-adverse action notice (copy of report + rights summary) if any adverse information is found; (e) provide final adverse action notice if the promotion is denied based on the report; (f) give the candidate an opportunity to dispute the report with the CRA. No FCRA requirement applies if no new background check is obtained; prior FCRA clearance at hire does not satisfy the obligation for a new promotion-gating check. Verify whether state law requires a background check for the new role; if yes, add to the promotion flow.

**4. EEO record-keeping (Title VII 15+, ADEA 20+, ADA 15+, EPA any size, PDA 15+, PWFA 15+, GINA 15+):** Employers within the coverage threshold for each statute must record and retain the promotion action (date, position, recipient name/sex/EEO category), stated reason, and pay change for at least one year. No specific federal form is mandated; the employer's internal record system is sufficient if it documents these facts. State EEO or pay-equity-reporting rules may impose additional detail (e.g., salary benchmarking, pay-ratio reporting); verify state law. The record is discoverable in EEO litigation.

**5. FMLA interaction:** If the promoted employee holds a protected leave balance (under FMLA, state leave law, or employer policy), the promotion and pay change do not reset, forfeit, reduce, or suspend that balance. An employee may not lose accrued leave or forfeited remaining leave as a consequence of a role or pay change. The employee remains entitled to the full accrual rate and use rights under the applicable leave law. If the new role has a different leave accrual or payout rule, that new rule applies going forward, but accrued-but-unused leave is preserved.

**6. NLRA § 7 anti-retaliation:** Ensure the promotion decision and its justification do not retaliate against union activity, concerted activity, or protected complaints (OSHA, discrimination charges, wage complaints). If the promoted employee engaged in protected activity within a reasonable lookback period (verify state anti-retaliation windows), document that the promotion decision was independent of that activity. Retaliation is an unfair labor practice and carries NLRB remedies (reinstatement, back pay, attorney fees).

**7. I-9 verification:** Exempt/non-exempt reclassification does not trigger I-9 re-verification. The I-9 certification stands and requires no update upon pay or role change, even if the change is substantial. However, if the promotion involves a change in legal name or visa status, verify whether re-completion is appropriate (consult USCIS guidance on Form I-9 updates).

**8. USERRA (uniformed services reemployment):** If the promoted employee is a veteran or current member of the uniformed services and the promotion affects accrued seniority, vacation, or pension vesting, ensure the promotion preserves reemployment rights as if continuous service had occurred. USERRA prohibits discrimination on the basis of military service and requires that returning service members are restored to the position they would have attained had they not been absent (with same seniority, status, and pay). Timing of military service relative to the promotion determines the scope of reemployment rights.

**9. Documentation best practice:** Record in the promotion file the date of the decision, the business reason, the salary/role before and after, the classification (exempt/non-exempt) before and after, the effective date, and the approver. If a background check was ordered, retain the FCRA disclosure, authorization, and notice copies. This documentation insulates against after-the-fact discrimination claims and NLRA retaliation claims.
