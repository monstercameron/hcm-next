# Georgia Employment Law Research

**State:** Georgia | **Researched:** 2026-09-03 | **Status:** REVIEWED

## 1. Summary for HCM Next

- **At-will employment dominates**: Georgia recognizes at-will employment by default except where illegal discrimination occurs; no written employment contract required at hire or on change.
- **Minimum wage defers to federal**: Georgia's statutory minimum (O.C.G.A. § 34-4-3: $5.15/hour) is superseded by federal FLSA ($7.25/hour); federal floor applies.
- **Semi-monthly pay frequency required**: O.C.G.A. § 34-7-2 mandates pay at least twice per month with roughly equal pay-period divisions; no state-specific final pay timing (federal FLSA + next regular payday practice).
- **No state overtime**: Federal FLSA overtime rules (1.5× over 40 hours/week) apply; no state-specific deviation or increase.
- **E-Verify mandate for 11+ private employees**: O.C.G.A. § 13-10-91 (as of July 1, 2013) requires private employers with 11+ employees to verify work eligibility via E-Verify; government contractors and license-seekers also covered.
- **Age discrimination 40–70**: O.C.G.A. § 34-1-2 prohibits discrimination against employees ages 40–70; compulsory retirement only allowed at 65–70 for high policy-making roles with $27,000+ immediate nonforfeitable benefit (pending HB1204 may expand to 40+).
- **Equal pay for sex**: O.C.G.A. § 34-5-1 to § 34-5-7 (Sex Discrimination in Employment Act) prohibits employers with 10+ employees from paying one sex less than the opposite sex for comparable work requiring the same or essentially the same knowledge, skills, effort, and responsibility; enforced by Georgia Commission on Equal Opportunity. Exceptions: seniority system, merit system, quality/quantity production, or factor other than sex.
- **Lactation breaks (paid)**: O.C.G.A. § 34-1-6 (effective 2020) requires reasonable paid break time and private location; exemption for employers <50 employees or off-site work.
- **Noncompetes enforceable with limits**: O.C.G.A. § 13-8-50 et seq. permits noncompete and nonsolicitation covenants; blue-pencil allowed but limited by 2023 case law (courts cannot add missing material terms).
- **Data breach notification required**: O.C.G.A. § 10-1-912 requires notification "in the most expedient time possible and without unreasonable delay."

## 2. Employment Relationship: At-Will Status and Written Notices

Georgia is an at-will employment state under common law and statutory recognitions. Unless a written contract specifies a term of employment, either party may terminate the employment relationship at any time, for any reason, or for no reason, **except** termination cannot be based on illegal discrimination (e.g., race, sex, disability under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act or the ADA).

No state law requires written notice to an employee at hire regarding at-will status, wage theft prevention, or pay-rate changes—these are governed by federal law (FLSA 29 U.S.C. § 206–214) and general contract principles. However, best practice is to provide: (1) an **offer letter** documenting the position, title, salary, start date, and at-will status; (2) an **employee handbook or employment agreement** specifying at-will employment (in Georgia, "either party may terminate the relationship at any time, for any reason, or for no reason"), compensation, benefits, leave policies, workplace rules, and any restricted covenants (noncompete, confidentiality); (3) **wage-rate notices** on hire and before any pay change (documenting the new rate, effective date, and pay frequency); (4) any **restricted-covenant agreements** (noncompete, nonsolicitation, confidentiality) signed before or at hire (with clear consideration, e.g., continued employment, access to trade secrets). Employers should document any at-will agreements and wage-rate changes in writing for audit protection and dispute resolution; no state-specific form or timing is mandated.

**Written employment agreements**: While Georgia enforces at-will employment as the default, employers may contract for a **fixed term** ("employment for 1 year") or **conditions of employment** ("termination only for cause"). Such agreements must be in writing, signed by both parties, and supported by consideration (e.g., a promise to employ for a term, special compensation, access to trade secrets). An employer cannot unilaterally impose a term; both parties must agree. At-will status is the default absent a clear written agreement specifying a term or conditions.

When an employee is separated, employers must file a **Separation Notice (DOL-800 form)** with the Georgia Department of Labor for unemployment insurance purposes, but this is an administrative requirement, not a worker notification. No statutory notice period to the worker is required beyond at-will principles (federal WARN Act applies only to mass layoffs of 50+ workers in covered employers with 100+ total employees).

**Jury Duty and Voting Protection**: O.C.G.A. § 34-1-3 prohibits employers from discharging, disciplining, or penalizing employees for jury duty attendance or responding to court orders; violations carry liability for actual damages and attorney's fees. O.C.G.A. § 21-2-404 permits up to 2 hours off to vote on election day or advance voting days, with employer discretion over timing; unpaid leave applies.

**Military Service**: O.C.G.A. § 38-2-280 requires reemployment of employees who leave for military service if they apply within 90 days after discharge, remain qualified, and the job still exists; entitles members to unlimited unpaid leave for active federal/state service and up to 6 months leave per 4-year period for service school or annual training.

## 3. Wages: Minimum, Overtime, Pay Frequency, Final Pay, and Deductions

**Minimum Wage**: O.C.G.A. § 34-4-3 sets a statutory minimum of $5.15/hour, but federal FLSA minimum ($7.25/hour) supersedes it; employers must pay whichever is higher. For all practical purposes, the federal floor applies.

**Overtime**: Federal FLSA rules apply; no state-specific deviation. Overtime is 1.5× regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek; executive, administrative, professional, and outside-sales exemptions follow FLSA definitions (29 U.S.C. § 213). No Georgia state overtime statute exists.

**Pay Frequency**: O.C.G.A. § 34-7-2 **requires payment at least semi-monthly** (twice per month) on dates such that the month is divided into at least two roughly equal pay periods. Exceptions exist for certain managers, farming operations, and sawmill employees (who may be paid monthly or annually by agreement); otherwise, semi-monthly is the minimum. Payment may be by lawful money, check, or (with written employee consent) credit transfer to a financial institution.

**Final Paycheck on Termination or Resignation**: **No state-specific timing statute**; federal FLSA and Georgia at-will principles require that all earned wages be paid in full and timely. No Georgia law mandates immediate final pay (unlike some states), but best practice and common contracts specify payment on or before the next regular payday (typically 5–14 days after separation). The employer's written pay policy controls; if the policy is silent, federal FLSA ("timely" payment) applies. **Final pay must include**: (1) all base wages through the last day worked, (2) all accrued overtime (calculated through the last day, not the end of pay period), (3) all bonuses or incentives earned (if contractual), (4) accrued paid leave (if the employer's policy or employment contract mandates payout), (5) any unused floating holidays or company-provided leave (verify employer policy). **Prohibited deductions from final pay**: The employer cannot deduct for uniform loss, tool breakage, cash-register shortages, training costs, or disciplinary reasons (such deductions are prohibited by federal wage law). Court-ordered withholdings (child support, garnishment) are permitted. **No Georgia statute mandates severance pay**; severance is contractual or discretionary. If severance is offered, document the offer letter and release agreement clearly.

**Deductions and Pay Statements**: **Federal FLSA** governs permissible deductions (only those required by law, court order, or authorized by employee in writing). No deduction may reduce wages below minimum wage or eliminate earned compensation. **Prohibited deductions** (common-law and federal wage principles): cash-register shortages, uniform damage or cleaning costs, tools, equipment, training fees, or "breakage" (any loss blamed on the employee). **Permitted deductions**: income-tax withholding (federal, state, local), Social Security (FICA), unemployment insurance (employee portion), court-ordered garnishment or child support, union dues (if authorized), 401(k) or retirement contributions (authorized in writing), health insurance premiums (employer-sponsored, authorized), voluntary savings or loan repayments (clear written authorization), and deductions required by law.

**Georgia-specific pay-statement requirement**: No specific statute mandates pay-statement content. **Federal FLSA** requires employers to maintain records showing amounts paid and pay periods but does not mandate detailed pay stubs. **Best practice** (not required but recommended to reduce disputes): Provide itemized pay stubs showing: (1) gross pay, (2) each deduction (tax, benefit, etc.) with amounts, (3) net pay, (4) hours worked (hourly employees), (5) pay period start/end dates, (6) year-to-date totals. Electronic pay stubs are permissible if the employee has access and can print/save a copy. Retain pay stubs for at least 3 years (federal FLSA) or 4 years (Georgia payroll-record best practice).

## 4. Pay Transparency and Equity

**Salary History Bans**: No Georgia state law restricts inquiries about salary history. Federal EEOC guidance discourages such inquiries, but Georgia has not enacted a categorical ban.

**Pay Range Disclosure**: No Georgia state law requires salary ranges in job postings. Federal rules (FLSA) do not mandate pay transparency in recruitment; this remains optional.

**Equal Pay Law**: O.C.G.A. § 34-5-1 (Declaration of Public Policy) through § 34-5-7 (Posting of Law by Employers) comprise Georgia's **Sex Discrimination in Employment Act**. § 34-5-3 (the operative section; verify exact citation) prohibits employers with 10+ employees from discriminating in wages on the basis of sex. Employers must pay equal wages to males and females for equal work in jobs requiring equal skill, effort, responsibility, and performed under similar working conditions. **Comparable work** means substantially identical job performance and qualifications, not identical job titles. Exceptions include seniority systems, merit systems, production-quality/quantity systems, or differentials based on factors other than sex. Enforced by the Georgia Commission on Equal Opportunity (GCEO). § 34-5-7 requires employers to post the statute in a conspicuous workplace location.

**Age Discrimination**: O.C.G.A. § 34-1-2 prohibits employers from refusing to hire, firing, or barring employees **aged 40–70** solely on grounds of age when the individual is qualified. Compulsory retirement is permitted only for those 65–70 in bona fide executive/high policy-making positions with at least $27,000/year immediate nonforfeitable retirement benefit. Violations constitute misdemeanor (fines $100–$250). Pending legislation (HB1204) proposes expanding protected age to 40 and older; verify current status.

**Pay Data Reporting**: No Georgia state law requires employers to report pay equity data. Federal EEO-1 reporting applies to employers with 100+ employees.

**Protected Classes**: Title VII, ADA, Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA), and other federal laws apply. Georgia enforces these through GCEO; no state-specific expansion of protected classes beyond federal standards found.

## 5. Leave and Time: Paid Sick Leave, Family Leave, and Other Mandated Leave

**No state mandate for paid sick or family leave** for private-sector employees. FMLA (federal, 12 weeks unpaid) applies to covered employers (50+ employees within 75 miles for qualifying reasons: serious health condition, family member care, military exigency, childbirth/adoption). Employers must maintain health insurance during FMLA leave.

**Lactation Breaks (Paid)**: O.C.G.A. § 34-1-6 (effective August 5, 2020) requires employers to provide **reasonable paid break time** and a **private location other than a restroom** for employees to express breast milk during work hours. No one-year time limit (unlike federal law); applies to all employers except those with fewer than 50 employees. Exception: no paid breaks required if employee works away from any employer worksite on that day.

**Jury Duty**: O.C.G.A. § 34-1-3 prohibits discharge or penalty for jury duty; no paid leave required. Employer may require reasonable notice and reasonable notification policies.

**Voting Leave**: O.C.G.A. § 21-2-404 permits up to 2 hours off to vote on election day or advance voting days with reasonable notice; employer may specify timing. Unpaid leave.

**Military Service**: O.C.G.A. § 38-2-280 requires reemployment and provides unlimited unpaid leave for active federal/state service plus up to 6 months per 4-year period for training.

**Holidays and PTO**: Entirely optional; no state mandate. Employers set policies and communicate them via employee handbook or offer letter. No state requirement to provide paid holidays, vacation time, or personal days.

**Leave Interaction with Pay/Role Changes**: When an employee receives a promotion or base-pay increase, existing leave balances are typically preserved unless the employer's written policy specifies otherwise (e.g., "all balances reset upon promotion"). Georgia does not mandate that paid leave be forfeited or paid out upon promotion; the employer controls this via policy. **Important**: If the employment contract or employee handbook states that accrued paid time off is "earned compensation," termination may trigger final-pay obligations to pay out the accrued balance (La. R.S. 23:631 principles, though Georgia final-pay law is less explicit). Employers should document leave policies clearly in writing, including: (1) how leave accrues (per-payroll-period, annual allocation at start of year, etc.), (2) whether leave carries over to the next year, (3) whether forfeited balances are paid out (use-it-or-lose-it policy must be stated clearly), and (4) whether promotion triggers reset, continuation, or partial payout. Ambiguity may expose employers to disputes or claims.

## 6. Records and Access: Retention, Employee Access, and Format Rules

**Payroll Record Retention**: **Federal FLSA (29 U.S.C. § 211)** requires at least **3 years** for payroll records (hours worked, wages paid, deductions). **Georgia O.C.G.A. § 34-4-5** requires employers to maintain records showing hours worked and wages paid and to make them available for inspection by the Department of Labor upon demand; the statute does not specify a retention period but requires records remain open to inspection. **Best practice and recommended retention: 4 years** after the calendar year in which remuneration was paid, as this aligns with statute-of-limitation windows and audit best practices. Retain records covering: (1) employee name, address, occupation; (2) hours worked per day/week; (3) wages paid; (4) deductions; (5) basis for wage calculations (e.g., hourly rate, salary). Records must be preserved in a format accessible to state investigators.

**Personnel Files and Employee Access**: **Georgia law does not grant private-sector employees a statutory right to access their personnel files.** O.C.G.A. § 50-18-70 (Open Records Act) applies only to public records held by government agencies, not private employers. Private employers may voluntarily permit employee access per company policy; access to recommendations and third-party evaluations may be withheld if confidentiality is promised. Verify: Check employment contract and company policy for any commitments to personnel-file access.

**Record Format**: No state requirement for paper vs. electronic. Employers may maintain records digitally provided they remain accessible and authentic (federal FLSA allows electronic records).

**Separation Records**: Employers should retain separation notices and final pay records as part of the 4-year payroll retention cycle.

## 7. Privacy and Data: Employee Data, Biometric Monitoring, Breach Notification

**Data Privacy Law**: Georgia has **no comprehensive state data privacy statute** equivalent to California's CCPA, Colorado's CPA, or GDPR. Employee data is regulated primarily by **federal law** (FCRA for background checks, HIPAA for health information, GLBA for financial institutions) and **Georgia common law** on privacy torts and trade secrets. However, **O.C.G.A. § 10-1-910 to § 10-1-912** impose **specific data-breach notification requirements**.

**Breach Notification (O.C.G.A. § 10-1-910 to § 10-1-912)**: When any person or entity, including an employer, that maintains unencrypted personal information (defined as SSN, driver's license number, credit card number, financial account number with security code, or health/medical record information) experiences a **breach of security** (unauthorized access or acquisition), the entity must notify **all residents whose unencrypted personal information was accessed or believed to be accessed** "**in the most expedient time possible and without unreasonable delay**" unless law enforcement requests delayed notification to avoid compromising an ongoing criminal investigation. **No specific day count** is mandated; "expedient" is the operative standard. **Industry best practice: notify within 3 business days** to establish "expedient" compliance. Notification must include: (1) the date of breach and description of compromised information; (2) steps affected individuals should take; (3) the entity's contact information; (4) information about credit-monitoring or fraud-alert services if offered. Breach notice must be delivered by mail, email, or telephone to the resident's last known address or phone number; if the entity cannot contact the resident, the entity must provide notice by publishing in a newspaper of general circulation in the area where the resident is believed to reside, or by posting on the entity's website if the website is regularly maintained. **No private right of action** exists; enforcement is by the Georgia Attorney General.

**Biometric Data Collection**: Georgia has **no state statute restricting or regulating collection, storage, or use of employee biometric data** (e.g., fingerprints for background checks, iris scans, facial recognition, voiceprints). Employers may collect biometric data for lawful purposes (background checks, time-clock systems, facility access) provided: (1) employees are given **clear advance notice** that biometric data will be collected, (2) **reasonable consent** is obtained (written authorization is best practice), (3) data is maintained securely and not shared with third parties without consent, and (4) collection is not discriminatory or invasive (e.g., no collection from restrooms or changing areas). **Federal ECPA Title I** and **Georgia common law on privacy tort** (invasion of privacy, unreasonable intrusion on seclusion) provide baseline protections if collection is undisclosed or unreasonably invasive.

**Electronic Monitoring and Workplace Surveillance (O.C.G.A. § 16-11-62)**: **Georgia is a one-party-consent jurisdiction** for recording communications. Under O.C.G.A. § 16-11-62: (1) **telephone recordings**: At least one party to a phone call must consent for recording to be legal; if the employer is a party to the call, the employer may record without notifying the other party (though best practice is to notify). (2) **video surveillance**: Employers may use video cameras in common workplace areas (lobbies, hallways, sales floors, warehouse) for security, theft prevention, and safety without employee consent, provided **cameras are not placed in areas where employees have a reasonable expectation of privacy** (restrooms, locker rooms, changing areas, private offices without notice). (3) **notice requirement**: Best practice is to **post conspicuous notice** in employee areas, in the employee handbook, or in an at-hire email that video surveillance, call recording, and computer monitoring may occur. Failure to provide notice does not make surveillance illegal if the area is not private, but notice reduces disputes and demonstrates transparency. (4) **email and computer monitoring**: No statute restricts employer monitoring of business devices or business emails on employer-provided computers or networks; monitoring of personal devices or personal email accounts is more restricted and should be avoided. (5) **keystroke logging and GPS tracking**: No specific Georgia statute restricts keystroke logging of business devices or GPS tracking of work vehicles; employers may monitor these, with notice. **Criminal penalties**: Unauthorized recording in private places (§ 16-11-62) is a felony (1–5 years prison and/or up to $10,000 fine).

**Employee Record Confidentiality**: Georgia does not impose state-specific employee data residency or cross-border processing restrictions. Employers may use cloud-based payroll systems, out-of-state HR software, and third-party data processors, but remain liable for vendor breaches under § 10-1-912 if personal information is exposed. **Vendor contracts** should include (1) indemnification clauses, (2) breach-notification obligations, and (3) data-security requirements (encryption, access controls, audit logs).

## 8. Hiring and Background: Ban-the-Box, Background Checks, Drug Testing, E-Verify

**Ban-the-Box**: Georgia has **no ban-the-box law**. Employers are permitted to inquire about criminal history on applications, during interviews, or via background check at any time. No recent legislative changes found. An employer may ask: "Have you ever been convicted of a felony?" or request authorization for a criminal background check at any hiring stage. Georgia does not impose the fair-chance (ban-the-box) requirement that some states do.

**Background Checks**: **Permitted under federal FCRA (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.)**. Employers commonly use criminal-history checks via the **Georgia Crime Information Center (GCIC)** (operated by the Georgia Bureau of Investigation) with fingerprints or name-based consent. No state law restricts timing or scope for most private employers; employers may conduct background checks before, during, or after the interview. **FCRA compliance requirements**: (1) provide a **clear, written disclosure** to the applicant/employee that a background check will be conducted (separate, conspicuous written notice, not buried in an application form); (2) obtain **written authorization** (signed consent) before running the check; (3) if adverse action is taken based on the report (offer rescinded, promotion denied, termination), provide a **pre-adverse action notice** and copy of the report with FCRA rights; (4) give the applicant/employee **at least 5 business days** to dispute inaccurate information with the background-check company; (5) provide a final **adverse action notice** if the employer takes action based on the report. **Individualized assessment** is required: employers should not use categorical exclusions (e.g., "any felony conviction disqualifies all applicants"); instead, assess whether the conviction is job-related (e.g., drug-trafficking conviction for a school teacher) or sufficiently old/rehabilitated. **Expungement/record sealing**: Georgia Code of Criminal Procedure Art. 215.1 et seq. allows expungement of certain arrests and convictions; applicants may truthfully deny expunged records, and employers cannot inquire about expunged convictions or use them in hiring decisions.

**Drug Testing**: No state law prohibits **pre-employment drug testing**. Employers may conduct drug tests at any time as a condition of hire (with notice and authorization). Federal rules (Americans with Disabilities Act, FLSA) apply: testing cannot target employees with disabilities, and medical testing must comply with medical privacy laws. **Workplace drug testing** (post-hire, post-accident, reasonable-suspicion) is permitted but should follow best practices: (1) provide written notice at hire that testing may occur; (2) use SAMHSA-certified labs; (3) confirm positive results with a second test (GC-MS or equivalent); (4) give the employee an opportunity to explain (valid prescription, false positive); (5) document all testing and results; (6) protect test results as confidential medical information. **Consult O.C.G.A. § 34-9-410 (Drug-Free Workplace Act)** for any additional state workplace testing requirements and employer liability protections; status as of 2026 should be verified by legal counsel. **Note**: Marijuana use for medical purposes is not protected under Georgia law; employers may prohibit marijuana use and test for it, even if the employee has a valid medical card from another state.

**E-Verify**: **Required for private employers with 11+ employees** (O.C.G.A. § 13-10-91, effective July 1, 2013). Employers must verify work eligibility through **E-Verify**, operated by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS). **Counting employees**: Count all employees working 35+ hours/week in Georgia (including part-time, full-time, temp, contract workers on the company's payroll). Once the employer reaches 11 employees at any point, E-Verify registration becomes mandatory. **Timeline**: E-Verify verification must be completed **within 3 business days** of hire (by the end of the third business day following the hire date). **Acceptable documents**: Employers should verify acceptable I-9 documents (passport, driver's license + SSN, etc.); E-Verify cross-checks with Social Security Administration and Department of Homeland Security databases. **Tentatative NonConfirmations (TNCs)**: If E-Verify flags a TNC (data mismatch), the employee has 8 federal work days to contest or provide documentation; employers must wait for case resolution before taking adverse action. **Not work authorized status**: If E-Verify determines "not work authorized," the employer must terminate the employee or face penalties. **Government contractors** (contract value >$2,499.99) and **license applicants** (certain professional licenses) must also certify E-Verify compliance regardless of employee count. **Failure to comply**: Exposes employers to Georgia Department of Labor audit, penalties (civil and criminal), and potential loss of business licenses. Some states impose penalties of $100–$1,000 per day of non-compliance per employee; verify Georgia's specific penalty structure.

**Negligent Hiring**: Georgia recognizes negligent-hiring liability; use of background checks and careful vetting mitigates risk. Employers who hire an employee without conducting a reasonable background check, if that employee later injures or damages a third party, may be liable for negligent hiring (even if the employment relationship itself is at-will). To mitigate risk: conduct background checks appropriate to the role (criminal history for public-facing roles, financial history for finance roles, driving record for driver positions), document the checks, document hiring decisions, and avoid hiring individuals with obvious red flags (prior violent convictions for security roles, embezzlement convictions for cashier roles, etc.) without a clear business justification.

## 9. Separation: WARN, Severance, Notice, and Noncompetes

**WARN Act**: The federal **Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act** (29 U.S.C. § 2102) requires covered employers (100+ employees) to provide **60 days' written notice** before plant closings or mass layoffs affecting 50+ workers in a 30-day period. Applies nationwide, including Georgia. Georgia has **no separate mini-WARN** statute; WARN is the only state-applicable mass-layoff notice rule.

**Georgia Mass Separation Notice**: In addition to federal WARN (if applicable), Georgia requires employers to file a **Mass Separation Notice (DOL-402A form)** with the Georgia Department of Labor **within 48 hours** when 25+ workers are separated on the same day, for the same reason, for a permanent/indefinite/7+-day separation (GDOL rules). This is an administrative requirement distinct from WARN and applies to smaller employers.

**Severance Pay**: No state law mandates severance. Severance is entirely contract-based or employer discretionary. If an employer chooses to offer severance (e.g., severance packages with severance pay, extended health insurance, outplacement services), the offer is not a legal obligation. However, if the employer has stated a policy (employee handbook, past practice, signed agreement), courts may enforce it as an implied contract. **Best practice**: If severance is offered, provide a written **severance agreement** that specifies: (1) the severance amount and pay schedule (lump sum or over time), (2) conditions (e.g., "in exchange for a general release of claims"), (3) confidentiality or non-disparagement provisions (if applicable), (4) continuation of health insurance (if offered), and (5) the employee's right to review with legal counsel or union representative (if applicable, provide 21 days for review and 7 days to revoke for older workers under ADEA). Document that the employee was given the opportunity to consult with legal counsel before signing.

**Notice to Employee on Termination**: No state-specific notice period required; at-will employment permits immediate termination without advance notice to the worker. Employers should follow any notice period specified in an employment contract, collective-bargaining agreement, or written severance policy. **Best practice**: Provide written termination notice to the employee, either in person, email, or registered mail, specifying: (1) termination date (effective immediately or a specified future date), (2) last day of work, (3) reason for termination (performance, conduct, RIF, health reasons, etc.), (4) final paycheck amount and delivery date, (5) accrued leave balance and payout (if applicable), (6) COBRA continuation coverage (if offered), (7) return of company property (laptop, badge, keys, etc.), (8) final benefits status (health insurance, 401(k), stock options, etc.). Document the notice delivery method and date in the employee's personnel file.

**Noncompete and Nonsolicitation Agreements**: O.C.G.A. § 13-8-50 et seq. (Restrictive Covenants Act, 2011) permits noncompete and nonsolicitation covenants but imposes strict enforceability requirements. **Enforceability requirements**: (1) **Legitimate business interest** (employer must demonstrate trade secrets, confidential business information, customer relationships, substantial relationships with prospective/existing customers, or substantial relations with the employee); (2) **Reasonable scope** (type of prohibited work—too broad "any competing business" may fail; must be limited to work that would directly compete or harm customer relationships); (3) **Reasonable geographic area** (should be limited to where employer actually does business or operates; "worldwide" restriction may be unreasonable for a regional business); (4) **Reasonable duration** (Georgia presumes 2 years post-termination is reasonable; longer periods require justification). **Blue-pencil doctrine**: Georgia courts apply blue-pencil (judicial modification) to overbroad terms, **but** a 2023 Georgia Court of Appeals ruling (verify citation and current status) limits blue-pencil: courts **cannot add missing material terms** (e.g., cannot add a geographic restriction if the agreement is silent on geography; cannot add duration if missing). This means agreements must explicitly include all restrictions (scope, geography, duration) or risk being wholly unenforceable. **Non-solicitation vs. noncompete**: Customer non-solicitation agreements (prohibiting employee from soliciting employer's customers) are generally more enforceable than employee non-solicitation (prohibiting employee from hiring employer's employees). **Consideration**: Agreement must be supported by consideration (employment, promotion, access to confidential info; offering a job alone to an existing employee may not be sufficient; continued employment may suffice if contract is signed at hire). **Drafting guidance**: To preserve enforceability, draft agreements with explicit scope (e.g., "sales of software to financial institutions"), explicit geography (e.g., "within 50 miles of Atlanta"), and explicit duration (e.g., "2 years post-termination"). Document the legitimate business interest (trade secrets, customer lists, relationships) in writing at time of enforcement to defend against invalidation.

## 10. Classification and Multi-State Rules

**Contractor vs. Employee Classification**: Georgia's **Employment Security Law (O.C.G.A. § 34-8-35)** defines "employee" and "employment" for unemployment-insurance purposes using a **common-law/control test** and the **modified ABC test** (effective July 1, 2022). Neither contract labels nor the parties' belief controls; liability is based on the **true underlying nature of the working relationship**. An individual is an "employee" unless: (A) the individual is **free from control or direction** (the employer does not direct when, where, or how the work is performed; no instruction manuals, schedules, or operational oversight); (B) the work is **outside the employer's usual business** or performed outside the employer's premises (e.g., an independent plumber hired to repair a building, not an in-house facilities manager); and (C) the individual is **engaged in an independently established trade or business** (has their own business entity, advertises services, works for multiple clients, owns tools and equipment, maintains separate office). **All three prongs** must be met for contractor status; failure on any single element suggests employee status. **Additional factors** (control over work method, provision of tools, integration into the business, permanence, permanence of relationship) support employee classification. Misclassification of employees as independent contractors exposes employers to: (1) **unemployment-insurance fraud penalties** and audit back-assessments, (2) **wage-and-hour audits** and claims for unpaid overtime and minimum-wage violations (federal FLSA + state law), (3) **income-tax withholding liability**, (4) **workers' compensation audit and penalties**, and (5) civil liability to the worker. The Georgia Department of Labor, federal IRS, and federal Department of Labor may investigate misclassification on audit. Written contractor agreements should explicitly reference all three ABC prongs and include disclaimers (no benefits, no workers' comp, independent business status, right to control timing/method, outside usual business).

**Remote Work and Multi-State Employees**: **No Georgia-specific rules** govern remote workers or telecommuters. Employers with Georgia-based employees working remotely within Georgia are subject to Georgia employment law (wages, overtime, leave, discrimination, breach notification, etc.); **federal FLSA applies to wages and overtime**. **State income-tax withholding** is required if the employee is working in Georgia (employee's tax residency) regardless of where the employer is located; the employer must withhold Georgia income tax and file the appropriate forms with the **Georgia Department of Revenue**. **Remote workers in multiple states** require multi-state tax withholding and compliance: if a Georgia-based employee works partly in Florida (no state income tax) and partly in Georgia, the employer must withhold Georgia income tax for Georgia-worked days. Multi-state employers must comply with all applicable state laws in states where employees work (wages, leave, discrimination, privacy, etc.). **No reciprocal income-tax agreement** exists between Georgia and other states; employers must withhold separately for each state. **Atlanta remote-worker withholding (verify)**: Check whether Atlanta or other Georgia cities impose local income taxes on remote workers; most do not, but verify current ordinances with the **City of Atlanta Department of Finance**.

**Reciprocity**: Georgia recognizes **only Georgia law** for employment disputes involving Georgia-based work. Out-of-state employment contracts may invoke choice-of-law provisions ("This agreement is governed by Florida law"), but if work is performed in Georgia, **Georgia statutes** (wage-and-hour law, discrimination law, breach notification, privacy protections) **apply to those portions of employment** regardless of choice-of-law language. Employers cannot contractually waive or avoid Georgia law for Georgia-based work.

## 11. Implications for HCM Next (P1A/P1B)

**Typed Obligations and Workflow Triggers for Promotion, Base-Pay Change, Manager Change, Termination:**

### Notice Requirements and Timing

- **Pay Frequency Notice**: No pre-hire notice required; § 34-7-2 pay frequency (semi-monthly minimum) is employer's choice. Document in onboarding materials.
- **Pay Rate Change**: No state notice period required; federal FLSA applies (payment next regular payday). Document change in advance; no Georgia-specific notice statute.
- **Wage Theft Prevention Notice**: No Georgia wage theft statute; federal FLSA applies. Post federal contractor notices if applicable.
- **Voting/Jury Leave Notice**: No pre-notice required; employees must provide reasonable notice of jury duty. No voting-leave pre-notice statute.

### Field Restrictions and Retention Rules

- **At-Will Status Field**: Required; mark "at-will" for all Georgia private employees unless written employment contract specifies a term.
- **Age Tracking**: Required for adverse-action audit trail; flag if separation decision involves employee aged 40–70 (§ 34-1-2 protected class).
- **Payroll Record Retention**: **Recommended 4-year retention** after calendar-year end (O.C.G.A. § 34-4-5 requires records of hours worked and wages paid to stay open to Department of Labor inspection but does not itself state a retention period; 4 years is best practice aligned to federal FLSA's 3-year floor plus a buffer — this is not the same statute as the § 34-7-2 pay-frequency rule cited elsewhere in this file). Enforce retention schedule in system; flag for deletion after 4 years + calendar year.
- **Separation Records**: Include separation date, reason, final pay date, final pay amount, accrued leave payout; retain for 4 years.
- **E-Verify Documentation**: Required if employer has 11+ employees (§ 13-10-91). Store verification status and date; flag if unverified on hire or termination.
- **Lactation Break Accommodation**: If employee is female and lactating, record accommodation request and private-location provision in role/HR notes; no payment deduction allowed.

### Promotion and Base-Pay Change Workflows

- **Equal Pay Audit Trail**: For pay increase/promotion decisions, capture job title, duties, knowledge/skills/effort/responsibility (KSER) level before and after (§ 34-5-3, Sex Discrimination in Employment Act). Document if role is comparable to opposite-sex colleague's role at same or higher pay. Flag if new compensation differs from opposite-sex peer in same/similar role by more than 5% without documented justification (seniority, merit, production, legitimate factor other than sex). Retain all pay-decision records for 4 years; include performance ratings, salary justifications, market-rate analyses, and promotion approval memos. **Comparable work test**: Two positions are comparable if they require substantially equal knowledge, skills, effort, and responsibility—not identical titles or duties, but similar scope and accountability. Examples: two project managers in different departments with comparable responsibilities should be paid equally; a sales associate and order-entry clerk with different responsibilities are not comparable.
- **Age Consideration Check**: Flag if promotion or pay change affects worker 40–70 (§ 34-1-2 protected age range); avoid discriminatory intent language in decision records. Document business rationale (performance, market-rate, skill-set, promotion cycle) separate from age. Never reference age, health, tenure relative to peers, or qualifications about "energy" or "adaptability."
- **Leave Balance Preservation**: If employee has accrued paid leave (PTO, sick, etc.) not mandated by state, preserve balance unless employer policy explicitly specifies payout/forfeiture on promotion. Policy should state: (1) whether leave carries forward, (2) if capped annual balance is applied, (3) if payout is required. Provide written notification to employee if promotion affects leave status.
- **Semi-Monthly Pay Frequency**: Ensure pay cycle aligns with § 34-7-2 requirements (at least twice per month, roughly equal periods). If employer changes pay cycle for this worker, verify advance notice (recommend 2-week minimum) to avoid wage-deduction claims and compliance disputes.

### Contractor Misclassification Detection

- **Classification Audit Workflow**: For workers classified as independent contractors, audit against O.C.G.A. § 34-8-35 ABC test: (A) is the contractor free from control/direction (no instruction, schedule, oversight)? (B) is the work outside the employer's usual business or off-premises? (C) is the contractor independently established (has own business, multiple clients, owns tools, advertises)? **All three must be met.** If any prong fails, flag as likely employee and recommend reclassification.
- **Misclassification Penalties**: Explain to the employer: Misclassification exposes the company to unemployment-tax audit penalties, wage-and-hour back-wage claims (federal FLSA overtime + minimum wage for prior 3 years), income-tax withholding liability, workers' compensation audit, and civil damages to the worker. Georgia Department of Labor and federal IRS may investigate on complaint or audit.
- **Contractor Documentation**: Ensure contractor agreements explicitly reference all three ABC prongs: (1) contractor has control over work method/timing, (2) work is outside usual business (e.g., hire-a-plumber for repair, not in-house facilities management), (3) contractor maintains independent business (written evidence: business license, separate tax ID, invoice to multiple clients, etc.). Agreements should state: no benefits, no workers' comp coverage, contractor provides own tools, contractor liable for taxes and insurance.

### Separation and Termination Workflows

- **Final Paycheck Timing**: No Georgia statute mandates immediate final pay; federal FLSA + next regular payday is standard. Confirm employer policy; document timing (e.g., "final check 5 business days after separation"). Georgia does not impose specific timing requirements, but delayed final pay can result in wage disputes or claims; best practice is to pay all earned wages within 7 business days of termination. Include all wages through the last day worked, including accrued overtime (calculated through final day), bonuses (if contractual), and accrued leave payout (if policy mandates).
- **Accrued Leave Payout**: Georgia has no statute mandating payout of accrued paid leave on termination. Employers may implement any policy: (1) pay out all accrued leave in the final check; (2) forfeit unused leave ("use-it-or-lose-it"), provided the policy was communicated clearly at hire and annually; (3) carryover balances to the next year with a cap. The employer's written leave policy controls; if the policy is silent or ambiguous, some courts may infer that accrued leave is earned compensation owed at termination. **Recommendation**: Explicitly state in the employee handbook: "Accrued paid time off is [earned compensation / discretionary benefit]. Upon termination, unused balances will be [paid out in final check / forfeited / paid out up to X days]." Include the policy in offer letters and at-hire documents.
- **Separation Documentation**: Retain in the separated employee's file: (1) termination letter or notice, (2) reason for termination (performance, RIF, conduct, etc.), (3) final pay calculation and check stub, (4) accrued leave balance at termination, (5) final paycheck delivery date/method, (6) any severance agreement, release, or non-compete signed. Retain all documents for 4 years after termination per payroll-record rules (O.C.G.A. § 34-4-5).
- **Mass Separation Trigger (25+ same day, same reason, 7+ days)**: If threshold met, file Georgia **DOL-402A (Mass Separation Notice)** with the Georgia Department of Labor **within 48 hours**. System must auto-flag at 24 employees to alert HR; this is distinct from federal WARN and applies to smaller employers.
- **WARN Trigger (100+ employees, 50+ affected, 30-day period)**: If employer has 100+ employees and termination affects 50+ workers in a 30-day period (or 500+ workers regardless of %), provide **60-day written notice** to affected workers, union representatives (if any), state dislocated-worker agency, and local workforce board. Failure exposes employer to back pay and benefits liability for each non-notified worker plus penalties. System must auto-flag at 40 affected employees.
- **Unemployment Insurance Filing**: Employer must file **Separation Notice (DOL-800 form)** with Georgia Department of Labor; system integration recommended. File within 10 days of employee termination or at next payroll processing. Include: separation date, reason for termination (layoff, resignation, discharge for cause, etc.), final wages paid, and whether employee is eligible for unemployment insurance. Accurate UI filing protects employer against benefit charges if employee contests separation reason.
- **Data Breach Contingency**: If worker's personal information (SSN, driver's license, financial account, etc.) is exposed in a system breach, trigger "expedient notification" workflow per § 10-1-912. Notify within 3 business days to establish "expedient" compliance, include detailed breach facts and mitigation steps, and document notification in the employee file.

### Noncompete and Restrictive Covenant Management

- **Noncompete Attachment**: If employer uses noncompete/nonsolicitation agreements (§ 13-8-50), attach to employment record and flag for legal review before separation. Record scope, geography, duration, and date signed.
- **Blue-Pencil Risk**: Inform employer that Georgia courts will modify overbroad restrictions, but cannot add missing material terms (2023 case law). Agreements should explicitly include all restrictions (scope, geography, duration) to preserve enforceability.
- **Scope Field**: Record the business interest protected (customer list, trade secrets, relationships, etc.); reasonableness requires legitimate business interest.
- **Geography Field**: Record geographic restriction (e.g., "Georgia," "Atlanta metro," "national"); courts cannot add missing geographic limits.
- **Duration Field**: Record duration (e.g., "2 years post-termination"); Georgia presumes 2 years reasonable; verify current case law.

### Leave and Accommodation Triggers

- **Lactation Break Request**: If triggered, ensure employer provides paid break time and private location (§ 34-1-6). Log each break request and employer's accommodation response. Exemption: <50 employees or off-site work only.
- **Jury Duty Summons**: No pay required, but no discharge permitted (§ 34-1-3). System flag: if employee marks jury duty on leave calendar, confirm employer cannot penalize.
- **Voting Leave Request**: Up to 2 hours unpaid (§ 21-2-404); employer may specify timing. Log request and timing; no pay reduction.
- **Military Service Call**: If employee activates military leave, initiate reemployment protocol; record 90-day reemployment deadline (§ 38-2-280).

### E-Verify and Immigration Compliance

- **E-Verify Status**: Record verification date, status (authorized / tentative / not work authorized / cannot determine). If employer has 11+ employees, E-Verify is mandatory; flag non-compliance.
- **I-9 Documentation**: Retain I-9 forms for 3 years after hire or 1 year after termination, whichever is longer (federal). If E-Verify status is "not work authorized," employer must terminate; system should flag for immediate action.

### Age Discrimination Safeguards

- **Protected Age Notification**: When processing separation, pay change, or role change affecting workers 40–70, flag for age-discrimination review (§ 34-1-2). Document legitimate business reasons for decision (RIF, performance, restructure, etc.) separate from age.
- **Decision Documentation**: Require manager to record reason for separation/pay change in structured fields (not free text prone to bias); system should review for age-related language ("too expensive," "new blood," "out of touch," etc.).

### Data Privacy and Breach Notification Workflows

- **Breach Notification Trigger**: If unencrypted personal information is exposed (SSN, driver's license, credit card, financial account, health record), trigger breach-notification workflow immediately. O.C.G.A. § 10-1-912 requires notification "in the most expedient time possible and without unreasonable delay." **System recommendation: Notify affected individuals within 3 business days** to establish expedient compliance and minimize legal exposure.
- **Notification Content**: Include (1) date and nature of breach, (2) specific types of personal information exposed or believed exposed, (3) any known misuse of the information, (4) steps the individual should take (freeze credit, monitor accounts, activate fraud alert), (5) employer/company contact information, (6) offer of credit-monitoring or fraud-alert services if employer provides them. Notification by mail, email, or phone to last known address/contact.
- **Affected Population**: Must notify all Georgia residents whose personal information was breached, whether or not they are current employees. Include former employees, applicants, dependents (if health data included), and contractors.
- **Law Enforcement Delay**: If law enforcement requests delayed notification to avoid compromising an investigation, document the request in writing and defer notification, but provide it within the timeframe law enforcement specifies (typically 30 days or investigation conclusion, whichever is sooner).
- **Attorney General Reporting**: § 10-1-912 does not require reporting to the Georgia Attorney General, but best practice is to consult legal counsel on whether disclosure is advisable; the AG may investigate violations.
- **Vendor Liability**: If personal information was exposed through a third-party vendor (payroll processor, HR system, background-check company), ensure the vendor has indemnified the employer and is jointly notifying affected individuals. Verify vendor breach-notification obligations in the service agreement.
- **Monitoring and Recording Policies**: Document workplace surveillance policies (video, email, call recording, keystroke logging, GPS tracking) in the employee handbook. Provide notice at hire and via posted signs in monitored areas. Reference O.C.G.A. § 16-11-62 one-party-consent rule for phone calls; state that employer may record with consent (implied in handbook). For biometric data collection (fingerprinting, facial recognition, iris scan), obtain explicit written consent from employees and explain use (background check, time-clock access, etc.).
