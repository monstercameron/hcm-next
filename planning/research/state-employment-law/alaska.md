# Alaska Employment Law: State Requirements for Workforce Changes

**State:** Alaska | **Researched:** 2026-09-03 | **Status:** REVIEWED

## 1. Summary for HCM Next

- **Minimum wage:** $14.00/hour as of 2026-07-01 (July 1, 2026); scheduled to increase to $15.00/hour on July 1, 2027, then adjust annually for inflation starting January 1, 2028 based on Anchorage CPI-U (AS § 23.10.065). No tipped credit allowed; server minimum = regular minimum wage.
- **Exempt salary threshold:** White-collar exemptions require minimum weekly pay of $1,120 as of July 1, 2026, increasing to $1,200 on July 1, 2027, then adjusting annually with minimum wage (AS § 23.10.055(b)).
- **Overtime:** 1.5x regular rate for hours > 40/week or > 8/day (AS § 23.10.070); applies to all employers regardless of size. Narrow occupational exemptions: agricultural workers, seamen, employees of businesses with fewer than 4 employees, and other specific classifications. No executive/administrative exemption for businesses under 4 employees.
- **Paid sick leave (Ballot Measure 1):** Mandatory effective July 1, 2025; employees accrue 1 hour per 30 hours worked, capped at 40 hours annually (employers < 15 FTE) or 56 hours (employers ≥ 15 FTE). All accrued leave carries over year to year; rehire within 6 months requires reinstatement of previous balance. Not paid out on termination unless employer has a voluntary policy (AS §§ 23.10.066–.067).
- **Captive audience ban (Ballot Measure 1):** Effective July 1, 2025, employers cannot require employees to attend meetings primarily about political or religious matters (opinions on legislation, elections, unionization, or religious practices). Retaliation for refusal is prohibited (AS § 23.10.135).
- **At-will employment:** Default rule; no state notice requirement unless contract, handbook, or implied agreement says otherwise; Alaska courts recognize public-policy exceptions and implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing (AS §§ 23.10.100–.110).
- **Final pay:** All wages due within 3 working days of employer-initiated termination; within 3 days of the next regular payday if employee-initiated (minimum 3 days after notice received) (AS § 23.05.140).
- **Pay frequency and notice:** Semi-monthly minimum or more frequent; employers must notify employees in writing at hire and before any change of pay day, place, rate, and pay period (AS § 23.05.140).
- **Background checks and hiring:** No state ban-the-box law (except Anchorage municipal code § 5.20 for city employers); employers may ask about criminal history at any stage; no state limit on conviction reporting age.
- **Non-competes:** No state statute; Alaska courts will enforce if deemed "reasonable" and necessary to protect legitimate business interests (trade secrets, confidential information, customer relationships); case-by-case assessment on duration, geography, and scope (verify current Alaska case law).
- **Remote work:** Alaska has no state income tax, eliminating state withholding for remote workers. Employers must withhold federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and Alaska unemployment insurance. Unique: Alaska requires employer SUTA tax withholding from employee wages (unlike most states where only employers contribute).
- **Pay transparency:** No state law mandating salary-range disclosure in job postings or prohibiting salary-history inquiries; only federal Equal Pay Act and Title VII discrimination rules apply.

## 2. Employment Relationship

**At-will employment:** Alaska follows the presumption that employment is at-will, meaning either party can terminate for any lawful reason without cause or advance notice (Alaska Statutes § 23.10.100–23.10.110). Exceptions include:

- **Public policy exception:** Wrongful discharge claims if termination violates public policy (e.g., retaliation for workers' compensation claims, jury service, or reporting legal violations).
- **Implied contract:** Company handbook, written policy, or verbal assurances suggesting job security or termination-for-cause-only language may create an implied contract limiting the at-will presumption.
- **Implied covenant:** Alaska courts recognize an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing in all contracts, including employment.

**Discrimination and retaliation:** Alaska Human Rights Act (AS § 18.80.220 et seq.) prohibits discrimination on the basis of protected classes. Protected classes include:

- Race, color, national origin, religion, sex
- Age (applies to all employees; federal ADEA covers 40+)
- Disability (physical or mental impairment)
- Marital status and changes in marital status
- Pregnancy, childbirth, and related medical conditions
- Parenthood/family status
- Veteran status and military-connected status

The Alaska Commission for Human Rights administers and enforces these protections. Employers cannot retaliate against employees for opposing discriminatory practices, participating in investigations, filing complaints, or exercising statutory rights (workers' compensation claims, jury duty, whistleblower/public-policy protections). Retaliation is unlawful regardless of underlying discrimination merit. Employers with 2 or more employees must comply with harassment protections; discrimination protections apply to employers with 15 or more employees.

**Notice at hire:** Employers must provide all employees, in writing, at the time of hire:

- Day and place of pay
- Rate of pay (Alaska Statute § 23.05.140)

**Notice of pay or role changes:** Employers must provide written notice of any change to pay day, place, or rate before the next pay period is processed (AS § 23.05.140).

No state statute requires advance notice to employees before a termination; the federal WARN Act (60 days) applies only to employers with 100+ employees experiencing a mass layoff or plant closing.

## 3. Wages

**Minimum wage:** As of July 1, 2026, $14.00/hour (AS § 23.10.065(b)). Effective July 1, 2025, minimum wage became $13.00/hour; increases to $15.00/hour on July 1, 2027. Starting January 1, 2028, annual adjustments for inflation using the Anchorage CPI-U (Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers). Statute requires Alaska minimum wage to remain at least $2.00/hour higher than the federal minimum wage. School bus drivers must receive 2× minimum wage.

**No tip credit:** Employers may not credit tips or gratuities toward the minimum wage obligation (AS § 23.10.065(d)); differs from federal FLSA. All tips and gratuities belong to the employee.

**Overtime threshold and calculation:** Compensation at 1.5× regular rate for hours exceeding 40 per week or 8 per day, whichever is greater (AS § 23.10.070(b)). Applies to all employers regardless of size. Narrow exemptions include agricultural workers, seamen, employees of businesses with fewer than 4 employees, and certain occupational categories defined in Alaska Administrative Code § 8 AAC 15.320 (verify specific exemptions for particular roles).

**Pay frequency and notice requirements:** Employers must pay employees at least semi-monthly (AS § 23.05.140); more frequent payment is permitted. Upon hire and before any change, employers must provide written notice of payday, place of payment, rate of pay, and pay period start/end dates. Change notice must be given before the affected pay period.

**Final pay on termination:** If the employer terminates, all wages due immediately and payable within 3 working days (weekends, holidays, and termination day excluded from the count). If the employee terminates/resigns, wages due at the next regular payday at least 3 days after the employer receives notice of resignation (AS § 23.05.140(f)). Failure to timely pay triggers a penalty of regular wages from demand until paid, capped at 90 working days (AS § 23.05.140(g)). Calculation of 3-working-day deadline excludes the termination day itself.

**Permitted deductions:** Alaska Statutes do not explicitly itemize permissible deductions. Federal standards apply; unlawful deductions that reduce wages below minimum wage are prohibited. Common allowable deductions include taxes, Social Security, Medicare, insurance premiums, and court-ordered garnishments. Deductions for uniforms, tools, damage liability, or other business costs must not reduce wages below minimum wage (verify specific deductions allowable under Alaska law in case law).

**Pay statement content and form:** Employers must provide written or electronic pay stubs (AS § 23.05.140(d)) showing:

- Straight-time and overtime hours worked (each pay period)
- Rate(s) of pay (regular and overtime)
- Gross wages earned
- All tax deductions and other authorized deductions (itemized)
- Beginning and ending dates of the pay period
- Employer name, address, and pay period dates
- Current sick-leave balance accumulated and used (mandatory as of July 1, 2025; 8 AAC 15.160(h))

Electronic pay stubs are acceptable if employee can access and retain them.

## 4. Pay Transparency and Equity

**Salary-range disclosure:** No state requirement to disclose salary ranges in job postings, in response to employee requests, or to applicants. Alaska employers are not prohibited from inquiring about or relying on prior salary history when setting wages. No state pay-transparency law requiring disclosure of salary bands, internal equity audits, or pay-data reporting. (Verify if proposed Alaska Salary Transparency Act or wage-disclosure law has been enacted since research date 2026-09-03.)

**Salary history ban:** No state statute banning salary-history inquiries or prohibiting reliance on prior salary (unlike Colorado, California, Illinois, etc.). Employers may use prior salary as a factor in determining new compensation.

**Equal pay and discrimination:** Alaska Human Rights Act (AS § 18.80.200 et seq.) prohibits discrimination on the basis of sex in wages and employment. For equal-pay claims: employers must have a valid business reason (other than sex) for pay differentials between similarly situated employees. Federal Equal Pay Act (29 U.S.C. § 206(d)) and Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(h)) also apply and provide similar protections. Alaska recognizes no additional pay-equity or pay-transparency obligations beyond federal law; no state mandate for pay-data reporting, pay-equity audits, or demographic pay-gap analysis.

**Protected classes and discrimination claims:** Alaska law prohibits pay discrimination based on: race, color, national origin, religion, sex, age (all ages, not limited to 40+), disability, marital status and changes in marital status, pregnancy or parenthood or family status, and veteran status. Employers with 2 or more employees must comply with harassment and retaliation protections. Employers with 15 or more employees must comply with discrimination protections (hiring, firing, compensation, promotion, terms and conditions). Sexual harassment and harassment on any protected-class basis are unlawful. Retaliation for filing a complaint or participating in an investigation is also unlawful and may subject employers to separate liability.

## 5. Leave and Time

**Paid sick leave (Ballot Measure 1, effective 2025-07-01):** All employees accrue 1 hour of paid sick leave for every 30 hours worked (AS §§ 23.10.066–.067). Annual accrual is capped at:

- 40 hours for employers with fewer than 15 FTE employees
- 56 hours for employers with 15 or more FTE employees

Salaried exempt employees are presumed to work 40 hours per week for accrual purposes. Employers may front-load the full annual allotment upfront instead of accruing by-period; if front-loaded, it must be available on the first day of employment. All unused accrued leave carries over to the next calendar year; employers cannot reset the accrual clock annually. Permitted uses: employee's or family member's injury, illness, medical care (including preventive care), domestic violence-related legal services, sexual assault-related services, and stalking-related services. Employees cannot be required to find a replacement before using sick leave; notice requirements limited to what is feasible and reasonable.

**Payout of sick leave:** Employers are not required to pay out accrued sick leave upon termination, layoff, or resignation unless the employer has voluntarily chosen to implement a cash-out policy. However, if an employee is rehired within 6 months of separation and did not receive payment for accrued leave, the employer must reinstate and credit the previously accrued balance (AS § 23.10.066).

**Interaction with other leave:** Paid sick leave is separate from and does not replace vacation, personal days, or other paid time off. An employer may not require an employee to use vacation or other PTO before using sick leave.

**Employer notification and tracking:** Employers must inform employees of paid sick leave accrual rate, annual limits, and permitted uses at hire and annually thereafter. The current sick-leave balance must be displayed on each pay stub (or be accessible on-demand).

**Paid family and medical leave:** No state mandate beyond the mandatory paid sick leave. Federal FMLA applies if employer has 50+ employees within 75 miles. No state requirement for additional family or medical leave beyond sick leave accrual.

**Other mandated leave:** No state statute mandating jury duty, voting, or military service leave; federal rules apply where applicable. Employers cannot retaliate against employees for jury duty or time served.

## 6. Records and Access

**Payroll record retention:** Employers must keep accurate payroll records for at least 3 years, including employee name, address, occupation, daily and weekly hours worked, wages paid each pay period, and deductions made (Alaska Statute § 23.05.080). Records must be sufficient to document compliance with minimum wage, overtime, and paid sick leave obligations.

**Employee access to personnel files:** Employees and former employees have a statutory right to inspect and copy their own personnel files and personnel information maintained by the employer during reasonable business hours under reasonable rules (AS § 23.10.430). Employers may charge the reasonable actual cost of duplication and copying; no statutory fee cap, but charges must not exceed actual costs incurred. Employer may establish reasonable procedures (advance notice, frequency limits on requests) provided they do not circumvent the access right. Records subject to access include employment applications, performance reviews, disciplinary records, wage records, training records, and any other documents in the personnel file.

**Collective bargaining exception:** AS § 23.10.430 does not override collective bargaining agreements; union contracts may impose different terms.

**Record format requirements:** No mandatory format requirement; electronic and paper records are both acceptable. If employer maintains electronic records, employees must be able to access and review them in a readable format during business hours (verify if specific format requirements exist for electronic records).

**Sick-leave record requirements:** Employers must track sick-leave accrual (hours earned per pay period), usage (hours taken), and current balance for each employee. Current sick-leave balance must be displayed on each pay stub or made available to the employee on request (mandatory as of July 1, 2025; 8 AAC 15.160(h)). Sufficient detail to verify compliance with accrual caps and usage permissions required.

## 7. Privacy and Data

**State privacy law:** Alaska does not have a comprehensive state consumer data privacy law equivalent to CCPA or GDPR. Constitutional privacy rights under Article I Section 22 of the Alaska Constitution provide some protection against unreasonable government intrusion but do not generally restrict private-employer data practices.

**Biometric data:** Alaska Statutes § 18.13.200–270 contain targeted rules for biometric identifiers (fingerprints, voice patterns, retina/iris scans, face recognition). Employers collecting biometric data must inform employees of the type of biometric information collected, how it will be used, stored, and destroyed, and obtain prior written consent. Biometric data is NOT covered by the general data breach notification statute (AS § 45.48.010) but has separate statutory protections.

**Data breach notification:** Any entity (including employers) collecting personal information must notify affected Alaska residents of a breach of unencrypted personal information "in the most expeditious time possible and without unreasonable delay" (AS § 45.48.010). Notification method: first-class mail, email (if employee agreed to electronic notice), telephone, or prominent website posting. Unofficial timeline guidance suggests notification within 60 days of discovery. If breach affects 1,000 or more Alaska residents, must notify the Alaska Attorney General. Entities with 10 or fewer employees at the time of breach may be exempt from notification requirement. Breach notification must include: nature of the breach, estimated dates affected, and contact information for the entity and credit-monitoring resources.

**Employee data security and monitoring:** No state statute specifically mandating employer monitoring restrictions, eavesdropping consent, or employee data security protocols. Employers must comply with federal standards (FCRA for background checks, HIPAA for health data, federal wiretapping statutes for electronic monitoring). Common-law privacy rights may restrict electronic monitoring in areas of reasonable privacy expectation (bathrooms, changing rooms).

**Personnel file confidentiality:** Employee personnel records maintained by employers are subject to access rights under AS § 23.10.430 (described in Section 6). Records maintained by state agencies for state employees have separate confidentiality protections.

## 8. Hiring and Background

**Ban-the-box (limited):** No statewide private-sector ban-the-box law in Alaska. Exception: Anchorage Municipal Code § 5.20 prohibits city employers and contractors from inquiring about or considering criminal convictions before a conditional job offer has been extended. After the conditional offer stage, the employer may conduct a criminal background check and may consider the results in hiring decisions, subject to individualized assessment of conviction relevance to the job.

**Background checks and reporting:** Alaska has no state law limiting the types of information that may be screened or how old criminal convictions can be reported. Under federal standards, non-convictions (arrests, charges without conviction) may be reported for up to 7 years; convictions may be reported indefinitely unless expunged or sealed. Employers must comply with federal Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) when using third-party screening agencies (provide notice, obtain consent, allow dispute).

**Expungement and record access:** Employees may petition to expunge or seal criminal records in Alaska under Alaska Statutes § 12.55 if certain conditions are met (e.g., acquittal, charge dismissal, completion of probation). Once expunged, the employee may lawfully state that no conviction occurred.

**Drug testing:** No state statute regulating or restricting drug testing in private employment. Federal FCRA standards apply to third-party screening; pre-employment testing is generally permissible. Post-employment, reasonable-suspicion, and random testing (for certain safety-sensitive positions) are permissible. No statutory safe harbor provision exists; employers should document business justification and comply with FCRA when using third-party labs.

**E-Verify:** No state mandate for private employers. Alaska allows participation in the federal E-Verify system but participation is not required except for federal contractors and certain government-funded positions.

**Conviction consideration policy:** No state statute limiting consideration of criminal history; courts may apply public-policy principles in discrimination or wrongful discharge claims. If an employer applies a blanket conviction ban or fails to individualize consideration, an applicant may challenge as discriminatory if conviction history correlates with protected class.

## 9. Separation

**WARN Act:** Federal Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act applies (60-day notice for employers with 100+ employees; mass layoff = 50+ or 500+ employees depending on threshold). Alaska has no state mini-WARN. Violations incur liability for back pay and benefits up to 60 days per affected employee.

**Severance:** No state statute requiring severance; voluntary severance is a contractual matter.

**Notice requirements:** No state statute mandating advance notice before termination for at-will employees. Exception: contracts or handbooks may impose notice obligations.

**Non-compete agreements:** Alaska has no statute regulating non-compete agreements; common law applies. Courts will enforce if deemed reasonable in duration, geography, and scope and necessary to protect legitimate business interests (e.g., trade secrets, confidential business information, customer relationships, or ongoing business development). Reasonableness is assessed case-by-case considering: (1) protectable interest, (2) time period (typically 1–2 years considered reasonable, longer periods disfavored), (3) geographic scope (must be limited to area where employer actually conducts business or customer relationships exist), and (4) nature of the employee's role. Courts may "blue pencil" (modify) overbroad terms to make them enforceable if the overly restrictive language is severable. No statutory safe harbor; non-compete enforceability depends on judicial assessment of reasonableness at time of dispute.

**Non-solicitation agreements:** Enforceable if reasonable. Courts apply similar analysis to non-competes regarding scope, duration, and geographic limitation. Non-solicitation of employees and customer non-solicitation (preventing recruitment or solicitation of customers/clients) are both evaluated for reasonableness. Some Alaska courts disfavor customer non-solicitation as unduly restrictive compared to trade secret protection.

**Garden-leave and forfeiture clauses:** Alaska courts may enforce garden-leave provisions (paying an employee not to work for a competitor) if reasonable. Forfeiture of unvested benefits or severance for violation of a non-compete or non-solicitation may be enforceable if tied to legitimate business interests.

**Implied covenant of good faith:** Alaska courts recognize an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing in all contracts, including employment agreements. Discharge in bad faith or for a malicious motive—including enforcing non-competes unreasonably or in bad faith—may expose the employer to tort liability for breach of implied covenant (verify applicability to non-compete enforcement context).

## 10. Classification and Multi-State

**Contractor classification test (ABC test):** Alaska Statute § 23.20.525(a)(8)(A–C) applies the ABC test for unemployment-insurance and workers'-compensation purposes. All three prongs must be satisfied to classify a worker as an independent contractor; failure to meet any prong results in employee classification (presumption of employment). The three prongs are:

- **(A) Control:** Worker is free from control and direction by the hiring entity in performing the work (in fact and by agreement)
- **(B) Usual course:** Work is outside the usual course of the hiring entity's business (i.e., not integral to the business operations)
- **(C) Independent trade:** Worker is engaged in an independently established trade or business of the same type as the work being performed (offers services to the public, maintains own equipment/location, etc.)

All three prongs must be satisfied; Alaska does not provide an independent contractor exemption for specific occupations (unlike some states). Misclassification as a contractor exposes employers to back-wage liability, payroll-tax penalties, and workers' compensation assessments.

**Remote work implications:** Alaska imposes no state income tax, eliminating the need for state income-tax withholding even for remote workers living in Alaska. However, employers must withhold federal income tax, Social Security (6.2%), Medicare (1.45%), and Alaska unemployment insurance tax. Unique to Alaska: employers must withhold Alaska SUTA taxes (currently ~5.4% of wages) from employee wages and remit to the state, in addition to employer contributions (unlike most states where SUTA is an employer-only expense). This requirement applies to all employers with Alaska employees, even if physically based elsewhere.

**Multi-state compliance:** Employers with Alaska-based workers must comply with Alaska wage and hour law including minimum wage ($14.00/hour as of 7/1/2026), overtime (40/week or 8/day), paid sick leave (1 hr/30 hrs), pay frequency (semi-monthly minimum), final pay (3 working days), and all related notice requirements. Employers in other states with remote Alaska workers must register for Alaska unemployment insurance and pay SUTA contributions on Alaska wages.

**Reciprocity and comity:** Alaska has no reciprocity agreements with other states for unemployment insurance or payroll-tax purposes. Multi-state employers must register and comply separately in each state where they have employees.

## 11. Implications for P1A/P1B

**Promotions and base-pay changes:**

- **Written notice requirement:** Any change in pay rate, payday, or place of pay must be communicated in writing before the next pay period. HCM Next must support a pre-promotion notification workflow and log the date and method of delivery.
- **Sick-leave accrual continuity:** Promotion or role change does not reset sick-leave accrual; accrual continues at 1 hour per 30 hours worked. Platform must track and display current sick-leave balance at all times.
- **Overtime reclassification:** If a promotion or role change affects overtime exemption status (e.g., shifting to executive/administrative exempt role), the platform must validate exemption criteria and alert to the minimum-salary threshold ($1,120/week as of 2026-07-01 for exempt employees).
- **Pay frequency verification:** If a role change alters pay frequency, the platform must enforce the requirement that final notice goes out before the next pay period.

**Terminations:**

- **Final-pay timer:** Platform must flag terminations and enforce the 3-working-day deadline for employer-initiated terminations. Calculate deadline excluding weekends, holidays, and the termination day itself. Provide clear deadline date and penalty warning (regular wages from demand up to 90 working days if late).
- **Sick-leave payout determination:** Prompt user to confirm whether the employer has a voluntary sick-leave cash-out policy. If yes, calculate accrued balance and include in final check. If no policy exists, note that sick leave is forfeited on termination. Support rehire reinstatement if employee returns within 6 months.
- **Pay-statement accuracy:** Final pay statement must itemize all hours worked, wages owed, deductions, tax withholdings, and current sick-leave balance as of termination date (per 8 AAC 15.160(h)). Include beginning and ending dates of final pay period.
- **WARN Act flag:** If the termination is part of a mass layoff or plant closing affecting 50+ employees (or 500+ salary/hours depending on threshold), alert user to 60-day federal notice requirement (for employers with 100+ employees).

**Pay-change processing:**

- **Written notice requirement:** When processing a base-pay increase, decrease, or role change affecting pay, system must ensure written notice is generated and delivered to the employee before the next pay period is processed. Log the date, time, and method of notice delivery (email, print, signed acknowledgment).
- **Minimum-wage compliance check:** If role change or pay adjustment results in wage below minimum wage ($14.00 as of 7/1/2026), system should alert the user that it violates Alaska law.

**Compensation and equity:**

- **No salary-history restriction:** Alaska places no restriction on inquiring about or relying on prior salary in setting pay; platform need not enforce salary-history ban (unlike Colorado, California, etc.). HCM Next may include optional salary-history fields for context but is not required to restrict or hide such fields.
- **Equal-pay audit:** No state mandate for pay-data reporting, equal-pay audits, or demographic pay-gap analysis; federal Equal Pay Act and Title VII audits apply if employer conducts them. Platform need not include mandatory equal-pay reporting features for Alaska (though federal compliance tools remain useful).

**Sick-leave module requirements:**

- **Accrual tracking:** Platform must track sick-leave accrual at 1 hour per 30 hours worked and enforce annual caps (40 hours for <15 FTE, 56 hours for ≥15 FTE). Support both by-period accrual and front-loaded annual models.
- **Balance display:** Current balance must be displayed on every pay stub or be easily accessible by the employee on-demand.
- **Permitted uses:** Platform should provide controls to log sick-leave usage only for qualifying reasons (illness, medical care, domestic violence-related services); some employers may implement approval workflows.
- **Carryover tracking:** System must carry over all unused leave year-over-year; do not reset balances on anniversary date. Support rehire reinstatement if employee returns within 6 months.
- **No forced payout:** Platform should not automatically pay out accrued sick leave on termination unless employer configures an optional cash-out policy.
