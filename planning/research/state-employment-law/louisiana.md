# Louisiana Employment Law Research

**State:** Louisiana | **Researched:** 2026-09-03 | **Status:** REVIEWED

## 1. Summary for Human Capital Management Suite

- **At-will employment with statutory exceptions**: Louisiana recognizes at-will employment by default; however, public policy exceptions (illegal retaliation, protected activity, illegal discrimination) and some written contracts may limit this right. Exceptions include retaliation for jury duty, voting, workers' compensation claims, FMLA use, environmental whistleblower reporting, and protected-class discrimination.
- **No state minimum wage; federal FLSA applies**: Louisiana has no separate minimum wage statute; federal minimum ($7.25/hour) applies to all covered employees. No state overtime rule; federal FLSA overtime (1.5× for hours over 40/week) applies.
- **Final pay requirement: 15 days or next payday**: La. R.S. 23:631 requires final wages (including accrued leave, if applicable) to be paid within 15 days of termination or on the next regular payday, whichever is sooner; La. R.S. 23:632 imposes penalties for violations (interest and potential damages).
- **Pay frequency governed by contract; no state mandate**: Employers may set pay frequency (weekly, biweekly, monthly, etc.); no state law requires a specific schedule, but agreed frequency must be honored and documented (La. R.S. 23:633).
- **Wage deductions strictly limited**: La. R.S. 23:634–635 permit only deductions required by law, court-ordered, or authorized in writing by the employee; deductions for shortages, uniforms, or tools are prohibited.
- **Noncompete enforceability limited**: La. R.S. 23:921 caps noncompete agreements at 2 years in duration; agreements must be reasonable in scope and territory and demonstrate a legitimate business interest; 2024 Act 273 and verify 2025 amendments added physician-specific provisions.
- **Louisiana Employment Discrimination Law (La. R.S. 23:301 et seq.)**: Prohibits discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age (40+), disability, and other protected bases. Covers employers with 20+ employees (age discrimination); pregnancy discrimination under La. R.S. 23:341; enforced by the Louisiana Workforce Commission and the federal EEOC.
- **Database Security Breach Notification (La. R.S. 51:3071 et seq.)**: Requires notification to affected individuals "without unreasonable delay" if personal information is breached; industry best practice is 2–3 business days. No specific day count specified in statute.
- **Personal Online Account Privacy (La. R.S. 51:1951 et seq.)**: Prohibits employers from requesting passwords or accessing personal social media accounts; violation may result in civil liability.
- **Drug testing permitted with limitations**: La. R.S. 23:1017.1 et seq. permits pre-employment and workplace drug testing with notice, chain of custody, confirmation testing, and documented procedures. Post-accident and reasonable-suspicion testing allowed.
- **E-Verify mandate for public contracts**: La. R.S. 42:1451 and R.S. 38:2212.10 require E-Verify verification for public-contract employees and state-funded positions; private employers are not statutorily required to use E-Verify but may do so voluntarily.
- **No mini-WARN; federal WARN Act applies**: Louisiana has no state-level mini-WARN act; federal WARN Act (60 days for mass layoffs of 50+ workers) applies to covered employers (100+ employees).

## 2. Employment Relationship: At-Will Status and Written Notices

Louisiana is an **at-will employment state** under common law and **La. R.S. 23:1004**. Unless an employment contract specifies a term (e.g., "employment for 1 year") or conditions of employment, either party may terminate the relationship at any time, for any reason, or for no reason—**except** when termination violates public policy (e.g., retaliation for jury duty, voting, workers' compensation claims, protected-class discrimination under La. R.S. 23:301 et seq., or reporting illegal activity via **La. R.S. 23:967**, environmental whistleblower protection). The burden of proving an exception to at-will employment rests with the employee.

**No state-mandated at-hire notices**: Louisiana law does **not require** written notice to an employee at hire regarding at-will status, wage theft prevention, pay frequency, or scheduled deductions. Employers should voluntarily provide an employee handbook or offer letter documenting at-will status, pay frequency, benefits, leave policies, and any conditions of employment to demonstrate clarity and good faith; this also protects the employer in disputes.

**Wage theft and pay history notices**: Federal FLSA requires no specific notice, but employers should document wage rate, hours, and deduction authorizations in writing. Louisiana does not impose separate wage-history notification requirements beyond what the employment contract provides.

**Public policy exceptions to at-will employment**: La. R.S. 23:961 (political rights and freedom; restrictions forbidden), La. R.S. 23:964 (political coercion; prohibition on employer interference with political activity), La. R.S. 23:965 (jury duty protection), La. R.S. 23:967 (whistleblower protection for environmental, health, safety violations), **La. R.S. 23:1361** (workers' compensation retaliation), and federal law (FMLA, Title VII, ADA, ADEA) create protected-activity exceptions. Employers with 20+ employees are specifically prohibited from restricting or penalizing employees' political activities, voting, candidacy, or union membership under La. R.S. 23:961 and 23:964. **Termination within 90 days of protected activity** may be presumed retaliatory unless the employer shows a legitimate, documented reason unrelated to the protected act (e.g., performance, misconduct unrelated to the protected activity). The burden of proof shifts to the employer if termination follows protected activity within the 90-day window.

**Notice to employer upon termination**: No state law requires the employee to provide notice before resigning. At-will employment permits immediate separation from the employee's perspective. Best practice: employers should document the termination date, reason, final pay date, and whether the employee gave notice, to defend against wrongful-termination or retaliation claims.

**Employer obligations for promotions and pay changes**: (1) **Document the effective date** of the promotion or pay change; (2) **provide written notice** to the employee showing the new title, rate, and effective date; (3) **retain the notice** in the personnel file and in the employee's record; (4) **make no material negative changes** to pay without notice (e.g., reduce wages without discussion); (5) if the change triggers overtime or benefit changes, document those as well. No state-mandated notice period before the change takes effect, but best practice is at least 1 pay-period advance notice to allow the employee to adjust and to reduce disputes.

## 3. Wages: Minimum, Overtime, Pay Frequency, Final Pay, and Deductions

**Minimum Wage**: Louisiana has no statutory minimum wage; federal FLSA minimum ($7.25/hour) applies. Employers must pay whichever is higher: state minimum (none) or federal ($7.25/hour). For practical purposes, $7.25/hour is the floor.

**Overtime**: Federal FLSA rules apply. No state-specific deviation: overtime is 1.5× regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. Executive, administrative, professional, and outside-sales exemptions follow FLSA definitions (29 U.S.C. § 213).

**Pay Frequency**: La. R.S. 23:633 does not mandate a specific pay frequency. Employers may set their own schedule (weekly, biweekly, monthly, etc.) provided they honor the agreed frequency and comply with La. R.S. 23:631 (final pay timing).

**Final Paycheck on Termination or Resignation**: **La. R.S. 23:631** requires all earned wages, including accrued paid time off (if the employment contract or employer policy provides for it), to be paid within **15 days of termination** or on the **next regular payday, whichever is sooner**. Failure to comply triggers penalties under La. R.S. 23:632 (penalties, including interest and potential damages). Employers must calculate and pay overtime through the final day worked.

**Deductions and Pay Statements**: **La. R.S. 23:634** prohibits forfeiture of wages and any deduction that reduces wages below the minimum wage or effectively voids wage payments. **La. R.S. 23:635** permits only deductions that are (1) **required by law** (e.g., income tax, Social Security, unemployment insurance), (2) **court-ordered** (child support, garnishment, judgment), or (3) **authorized in writing by the employee** (union dues, 401(k) contributions, insurance premiums, voluntary savings, loan repayment). **Prohibited deductions** (common law and Louisiana law): "breakage" or cash-register shortages, uniform damage or cleaning costs, tools, equipment costs, training fees, meal costs, or any deduction designed to punish the employee or recover employer losses. If an employer advances a loan to an employee, repayment must be authorized in writing and cannot reduce the employee below minimum wage in any pay period; if repayment would reduce wages below minimum, the employer must delay repayment or adjust the schedule. **Pay-statement requirements**: No Louisiana-specific statutory mandate exists; federal FLSA requires only that records show the amount of wages paid and the pay period. **Best practice**: Provide itemized pay stubs showing (1) gross wages, (2) each deduction with amount and description, (3) net pay, (4) hours worked (for hourly employees), (5) YTD totals, (6) pay-period dates. Electronic pay stubs are permissible if the employee has secure access. Disputes over deductions should be documented in the personnel file and resolved in writing to avoid wage claims. Retain pay stubs for at least 3 years (federal FLSA requirement); Louisiana does not impose a longer retention period for private employers.

## 4. Pay Transparency and Equity

**Salary History Bans**: No Louisiana state law restricts inquiries about salary history or past compensation. Federal EEOC guidance discourages such inquiries in some contexts, but Louisiana has not enacted a categorical ban.

**Pay Range Disclosure**: No Louisiana state law requires salary ranges in job postings. Federal rules (FLSA) do not mandate pay transparency in recruitment; this remains optional.

**Equal Pay Law**: Louisiana has **no state-specific equal pay statute**, but discrimination based on sex in compensation is prohibited under **La. R.S. 23:301 et seq.** (Employment Discrimination Law). **La. R.S. 23:332** explicitly prohibits discrimination based on sex; **La. R.S. 23:341** addresses pregnancy-related discrimination. Federal **Equal Pay Act** (29 U.S.C. § 206(d)) also applies: employers must pay equal wages for equal work (substantially identical job performance) unless the difference is based on a seniority system, merit system, quality/quantity of production, or a factor other than sex. Defenses to pay discrimination claims include documented seniority, merit, production-based pay, or legitimate business factors (e.g., geographic location, role responsibility). The **Louisiana Workforce Commission (LWC)** and the **federal EEOC** enforce these statutes. Employers should conduct regular pay-equity audits, document pay decisions, and ensure pay is based on job duties, skills, and performance rather than gender, race, age, or other protected status.

**Pay Data Reporting**: No Louisiana state law requires employers to report pay equity data. Federal EEO-1 reporting applies to employers with 100+ employees.

**Protected Classes and Discrimination Law**: **La. R.S. 23:301 et seq.** prohibits discrimination in hiring, promotion, pay, discipline, and termination based on:

- Race, color, national origin (La. R.S. 23:301)
- Religion (La. R.S. 23:301)
- Sex, including pregnancy, childbirth, and related conditions (La. R.S. 23:332, 23:341)
- Age 40+ (La. R.S. 23:301; applies to employers with 20+ employees)
- Disability (La. R.S. 23:301; applies to employers with 20+ employees)
- Genetic information (La. R.S. 23:368)

**Pregnancy discrimination** (La. R.S. 23:341–342): Employers must treat pregnant employees the same as non-pregnant employees with similar work capacity (e.g., light-duty assignments, medical leave). Pregnancy-related conditions must be accommodated unless the accommodation poses an undue hardship. Federal Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg) also applies.

**Age discrimination** (La. R.S. 23:301): Applies to employers with 20+ employees. Protect employees 40+; avoid age-related remarks ("too old," "overqualified," "native digital," "new generation skills") in performance reviews or termination decisions. Federal ADEA (29 U.S.C. § 621) also applies to employers with 20+ employees.

**Disability discrimination** (La. R.S. 23:301): Employers with 20+ employees must provide reasonable accommodations unless undue hardship. Federal ADA applies to employers with 15+ employees.

**Enforcement**: La. R.S. 23:301 et seq. claims are filed with the **Louisiana Workforce Commission (LWC)**, which investigates and may order remedies (back pay, damages, attorney fees). Claims may also be filed with the federal **EEOC** for concurrent state/federal coverage. Statute of limitations is typically 3 years from the discriminatory act. Title VII, ADA, ADEA, and other federal laws also apply and run concurrently with state law; the EEOC or LWC may defer to the other agency.

## 5. Leave and Time: Paid Sick Leave, Family Leave, and Other Mandated Leave

**No state-mandated paid leave** (state preempts local leave mandates): Louisiana has **no state law requiring paid sick leave, paid family leave, or paid time off** for private-sector employees. Moreover, **La. R.S. 23:642** (Preemption of Local Employment Mandates) explicitly **preempts any local jurisdiction from establishing a mandatory, minimum number of vacation or sick leave days**. This means that even if a city (such as New Orleans) had previously enacted a paid-leave ordinance, it is preempted and unenforceable for private employers. Example: The New Orleans sick-leave ordinance (Ordinance 22,688) is preempted by state law and does not apply to private employers. **Federal FMLA** (12 weeks unpaid per 12 months for qualifying reasons: serious health condition, family member care, military exigency, military caregiver, childbirth/adoption) applies to covered employers (50+ employees within 75 miles). Employers must maintain health-insurance benefits during FMLA leave.

**Holidays and PTO**: Entirely optional; no state mandate. Employers may set policies and communicate them clearly at hire or in an employee handbook. If an employee is promoted or has a base-pay change, existing leave balances (if any) are typically preserved; no state rule specifies carryover or payout rules. Employers should be consistent in whether leave is forfeited at year-end ("use-it-or-lose-it"), rolled over to the next year, or paid out. The policy must be clearly communicated in writing to avoid disputes.

**FMLA Interaction with Other Leave**: If an employer offers paid leave (sick, vacation, personal days) and an employee takes FMLA leave, the employer **may require** the employee to use accrued paid leave to cover part or all of the FMLA absence (i.e., the employer may designate part of paid leave as FMLA leave). However, the employer **cannot reduce total leave balances** beyond what the employee accrues under the FMLA-substitution rule. The employee must return from FMLA leave with the same leave balance (minus the leave used during FMLA).

**Interaction with Pay/Role Changes**: If an employee is promoted to a new role, the employer should clarify whether accrued leave balances transfer with the employee or are forfeited. If an employee accrues paid time off and is terminated, **La. R.S. 23:631** (final pay) may require payout of accrued earned leave, depending on whether the employment contract or employer policy designates leave as earned wages or a discretionary benefit. If the employer's written policy states that accrued leave is "earned compensation" or "earned paid time off," final-pay law likely requires payout on termination. If the policy states leave is a "discretionary benefit" that may be forfeited, a court may allow forfeiture if the policy was clear at hire. Document the leave policy in writing and in the employment contract.

## 6. Records and Access: Retention, Employee Access, and Format Rules

**Payroll Record Retention**: Louisiana has **no statutory retention period for payroll records**; federal FLSA requires 3 years (29 U.S.C. § 211(b)). Employers must retain payroll records, time cards, work schedules, wage calculations, and collective-bargaining agreements for at least 3 years. Best practice: retain records for 4 years to account for statute-of-limitations variations and potential audits. Louisiana civil code does not impose additional private-sector payroll retention obligations beyond federal FLSA; however, employers should consult with legal counsel on industry-specific requirements.

**Personnel Files and Employee Access**: Louisiana has **no private-sector "personnel file access" statute** granting employees an automatic right to review their own employment records, unlike some other states. Common-law privacy tort principles (La. C.C. art. 450 et seq., unreasonable intrusion on seclusion) and La. R.S. 1:55 (right to privacy) provide general protections but do not require access on demand. **Best practice**: maintain transparent personnel-file policies, allow employees to inspect their own files (excluding evaluations of other employees, confidential sources, or legally privileged information) upon reasonable written request with 5 business days' notice, and document the review in the file. Employers may charge a reasonable photocopy fee if state law permits (verify current case law).

**Record Format and Authentication**: Louisiana has no state-specific requirement that records be maintained in paper form; digital records are permissible. Employers must ensure records are (1) authentic and tamper-proof (e.g., immutable logs or hashed files), (2) accessible for audit by federal FLSA investigators or Louisiana Workforce Commission, and (3) compliant with federal data-retention requirements. Digital records should be backed up, include metadata (date modified, author), and remain readable if the original software is obsolete (e.g., export to a standard format).

**Separation Records**: Maintain all separation records indefinitely or per document-retention policies. This includes: final pay calculation, separation notice, written confirmation of final check date and amount, unemployment-insurance separation notice filed with LWC, and any severance or non-compete signed by the departing employee. Verify whether the employer's policy requires notarization or witness signatures on separation agreements (state law does not require this, but best practice is to document consent).

**Records Related to Protected Activities**: Employers must retain records related to protected activity claims (workers' compensation, jury duty, FMLA leave, protected complaints, protected speech) for at least 3 years and must make them available to state agencies or the employee if a claim is filed. Destruction of records that could be relevant to a protected-activity retaliation claim may be considered spoliation or consciousness of guilt in litigation.

## 7. Privacy and Data: Employee Data, Biometric Monitoring, Breach Notification

**Data Privacy Law**: Louisiana has **no comprehensive state data privacy statute** equivalent to California's CCPA, Colorado's CPA, or New York's SHIELD Act that governs all personal information. However, **La. R.S. 51:3071 et seq.** (Database Security Breach Notification Law, effective 2018) imposes specific **breach notification obligations**. Federal privacy law (FCRA for background checks, HIPAA for health information, GLBA for financial institutions) applies where applicable. Employers should review data-handling procedures to ensure compliance with applicable federal and state laws.

**Breach Notification Requirement**: Under **La. R.S. 51:3071 et seq.** (effective 2018), when an employer or service provider that maintains or transmits unencrypted personal information (defined as a combination of data elements such as SSN, driver's license number, financial account number, credit card number, security code, or health/medical record information) experiences a breach of database security, the entity must notify affected residents **"without unreasonable delay"** unless law enforcement requests delayed notification (determine not to compromise criminal investigation within 30 days of notification). **No specific "day count" is mandated** by Louisiana statute; "without unreasonable delay" and "expedient" are the operative standards. Industry best practice is 2–3 business days. Notification must include: (1) the specific breach event and date; (2) types of personal information exposed or suspected to be exposed; (3) any known misuse of the information; (4) steps the affected individual should take to protect themselves; (5) the entity's contact information; and (6) notice of credit-monitoring or fraud-alert services offered at no cost if applicable.

**No Private Right of Action**: La. R.S. 51:3071 et seq. does not create a private right of action for individuals harmed by a breach; only the state attorney general may enforce. However, individuals may pursue common-law tort claims for negligence, invasion of privacy, or intentional infliction of emotional distress if the breach was caused by gross negligence or willful misconduct.

**Biometric Data Collection**: Louisiana has **no statute restricting or regulating collection, storage, or use of employee biometric data** (e.g., fingerprints for background checks, iris scans, facial recognition). Federal ECPA Title I (Wiretap Act and Stored Communications Act) and Louisiana common-law privacy torts (La. C.C. art. 450–457, invasion of privacy; La. C.C. art. 458, defamation; La. R.S. 9:2800–2803, malicious prosecution) provide some protections if collection is unreasonable or invasive. Employers should provide clear notice of biometric data collection and obtain consent (best practice); verify case law on "reasonable expectation of privacy" in the workplace context.

**Electronic Monitoring and Workplace Surveillance**: No Louisiana statute restricts workplace monitoring (e.g., email, internet browsing, keystroke logging, GPS tracking of work vehicles, or video surveillance in non-private areas). **Federal ECPA Title I** prohibits interception of wire communications (phone calls) and electronic communications (email) **without consent**; Louisiana recognizes federal protections. **Best practice**: (1) provide notice to employees that monitoring occurs (required to obtain legal consent); (2) limit monitoring to work devices and work activities (does not extend to personal devices or off-duty conduct); (3) avoid monitoring in areas with reasonable expectation of privacy (restrooms, changing rooms); (4) comply with ECPA consent requirements if monitoring involves communications.

**Employee Record Confidentiality and Data Residency**: Louisiana does not impose state-specific employee data residency or in-state processing requirements. Cloud storage, out-of-state payroll providers, and third-party HR systems are permitted. However, (1) employers remain liable for vendor breaches under **La. R.S. 51:3071** if they have contracted for data processing and a breach occurs; (2) ensure vendor data-processing agreements include indemnification and breach-notification obligations; (3) comply with federal privacy laws if applicable (HIPAA for health data, GLBA for financial data, FCRA for background checks).

**Social Media and Personal Online Account Privacy**: **La. R.S. 51:1951 et seq.** (Personal Online Account Privacy Protection Act) prohibits employers from (1) **requesting or requiring** an employee to disclose passwords or login credentials to personal accounts (social media, email); (2) accessing or attempting to access an employee's personal account without authorization; or (3) **taking adverse employment action** against an employee for refusing to provide access. Exceptions: employers may monitor business-account social media or conduct background checks using publicly available information. Violation may expose the employer to civil liability and statutory damages; verify current case law on scope and remedies.

## 8. Hiring and Background: Ban-the-Box, Background Checks, Drug Testing, E-Verify

**Ban-the-Box**: **Louisiana has no statewide ban-the-box law** for private employers. Employers are permitted to inquire about criminal history on applications, during interviews, or via background check at any time in the hiring process. However, **New Orleans (City of New Orleans) has a local ban-the-box ordinance** (verify: Ordinance 26,741 or City Code § 2-1120; effective date 2019) that applies to **public employers and city contractors receiving city funding or grants**. The New Orleans ordinance prohibits the City and its contractors from asking applicants about criminal history on initial applications; criminal background checks may only be conducted after an initial interview is offered. **Important**: The New Orleans ordinance applies **only to public employers and city-funded contractors**; private employers in New Orleans who do not have city contracts are **not subject** to the ban-the-box requirement. Private employers in New Orleans may still inquire about criminal history at any point in the hiring process.

**Background Checks**: Permitted under **federal Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)** (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.) with compliance requirements. Employers may use criminal-history checks, driving records, credit reports, civil litigation history, and employment verification through the Louisiana State Police, FBI (NCIC database), or third-party screening agencies. **FCRA Compliance**: If using a third party to conduct background checks, the employer must (1) provide clear, written notice to the applicant/employee that a background check will be conducted, (2) obtain written authorization before running the check, (3) provide a copy of the report and disclosure of rights if adverse action is taken, (4) give the applicant an opportunity to dispute the report before final rejection. **No state-law timing restriction** exists; employers may inquire about criminal history at any point in the hiring process (no ban-the-box law in Louisiana). However, best practice is to make individualized decisions about criminal records—avoid categorical exclusions (e.g., "any felony disqualifies"). **Certain industries** (healthcare, child care, security, financial services) may have additional restrictions or mandatory background checks under federal or state regulations; verify applicability for the specific role. **Expungement and record sealing**: Louisiana Code of Criminal Procedure art. 215.1 et seq. allows expungement of certain arrests and convictions; applicants may truthfully state that an expunged record does not exist, and employers should not inquire about or use expunged records.

**Drug Testing**: **La. R.S. 23:1017.1 et seq.** permits pre-employment, post-accident, and reasonable-suspicion drug testing. Employers using drug testing must (1) **provide written notice** at or before hire that drug testing is a condition of employment; (2) **follow chain-of-custody procedures** to ensure sample authenticity; (3) use **SAMHSA-certified or equivalent labs** for testing; (4) **confirm positive results** with a second test (e.g., GC-MS) before taking adverse action; (5) **give the employee opportunity to explain** a positive result (e.g., valid prescription); (6) **not discriminate** based on protected status (race, sex, disability). Post-accident testing is permitted if an injury occurs and the testing is done promptly. Reasonable-suspicion testing requires documented, articulable evidence of impairment or possession. A negative test result does not prevent discipline for unrelated workplace violations. **Certain industries** (e.g., commercial drivers, healthcare, federal contractors) may have additional or mandatory testing requirements under federal law or state regulations; verify applicability for the specific role or industry. Violation of testing procedures may expose the employer to tort liability or discrimination claims.

**E-Verify**: **La. R.S. 42:1451 and R.S. 38:2212.10** require **E-Verify verification for public-contract employees** and employees funded by state money. Private employers are not statutorily required to use E-Verify, but many do for compliance. Employers who use E-Verify must register with USCIS and must follow procedures (verify current enrollment and compliance).

**Negligent Hiring**: Louisiana recognizes negligent-hiring liability; use of background checks and careful vetting mitigates risk.

## 9. Separation: WARN, Severance, Notice, and Noncompetes

**WARN Act**: The federal **Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act (WARN)** (29 U.S.C. § 2102 et seq.) requires covered employers (100+ employees on payroll for 6+ months in prior 12 months) to provide **60 days' written notice** before plant closings or mass layoffs. **Covered events**: (1) plant closing (permanent shutdown affecting 50+ workers at a facility), (2) mass layoff (reduction of hours or termination of 50+ workers in a 30-day period), or (3) workforce reduction affecting 500+ workers regardless of percentage. Notice must be given to (1) the affected employees, (2) their union representatives (if any), (3) the state dislocated worker agency, and (4) the local workforce investment board. Failure to notify exposes the employer to civil liability (back pay and benefits for up to 60 days for each non-notified employee, plus attorney fees and penalties). Louisiana has **no state-specific mini-WARN act** (i.e., lower thresholds do not apply). However, employers should provide notice to state workforce agencies as a best practice even if not legally required under WARN.

**Severance Pay**: **No state law mandates severance.** Severance is entirely a matter of contract (written employment agreement or severance plan) or employer discretion. If the employer offers severance (e.g., severance packages, continuation of health insurance, outplacement services), the offer is not a legal obligation but should be documented in writing to prevent disputes. If severance is offered conditionally (e.g., in exchange for a release of claims), the agreement must be clear about the release scope, effective date, and confidentiality provisions (if any).

**Notice to Employee on Termination**: **No state-specific notice period required** under Louisiana law; at-will employment permits immediate termination without notice. However, (1) employers should follow any notice period specified in an employment contract, collective-bargaining agreement, or written severance policy; (2) best practice is to provide notice in writing (to the employee's home address or hand-delivered) specifying the termination date, final pay date, reason for termination, COBRA/health-insurance continuation information, and instructions for return of company property; (3) documentation of notice protects the employer in claims for wrongful termination or retaliation.

**Noncompete and Nonsolicitation Agreements**: **La. R.S. 23:921** permits noncompete agreements but imposes **significant enforceability limits**. Key restrictions:

- **Duration cap: 2 years maximum** from termination of employment (any longer term is void and unenforceable)
- **Reasonableness standard**: Agreements must be reasonable in scope (type of work prohibited), geographic territory (should be limited to where the employer actually does business), and the duration of the restriction must be justified by a legitimate business interest
- **Legitimate business interest**: Employer must demonstrate a protectable interest at the time of enforcement, such as trade secrets, confidential business information, customer relationships, or substantial relationships with specific prospective or existing customers (verify case law on what constitutes sufficient interest)
- **Consideration**: The agreement must be supported by consideration (e.g., employment, promotion, or access to confidential information); offering a job alone may not be sufficient if the employee is already employed
- **Physician amendments (2024 Act 273 and verify 2025 amendments)**: Recent amendments added parish-specific restrictions for physicians. Verify current physician-specific rules, including any limitation on enforcement in certain parishes or for certain medical specialties
- **Blue-pencil doctrine**: Louisiana courts apply the "blue-pencil" doctrine, allowing partial enforcement if an overly broad noncompete can be narrowed to reasonable scope without fundamentally changing the agreement; verify current case law on judicial authority to modify agreements

**Nonsolicitation agreements** (customer non-solicit, employee non-solicit) are also evaluated for reasonableness and legitimate business interest. Customer-based non-solicits are more likely to be enforced than employee non-solicits. Overly broad or indefinite agreements are unenforceable. **Disputes over enforceability**: Employers should be prepared to demonstrate the business interest and reasonableness of scope at the time of enforcement; witness testimony and documentation of the confidential information or customer relationships are essential.

## 10. Classification and Multi-State Rules

**Contractor vs. Employee Classification**: Louisiana's **Employment Security Law (La. R.S. 23:1471 et seq.)** defines "employee" and "employment" for unemployment-insurance purposes and wage-and-hour law. Louisiana applies the **ABC test** for independent-contractor classification: (A) the worker is **free from control** and direction in performing the work (not subject to instructions, schedules, or operational oversight by the employer; the worker controls the means and method of work); (B) the work is **outside the employer's usual business** or performed outside the employer's place of business (e.g., a plumber hired to repair a facility vs. a salaried facilities manager; courier service for a law firm vs. in-house delivery staff); and (C) the worker is **engaged in an independently established trade or business** (has their own business entity, advertises services, works for multiple clients, maintains their own tools/equipment, maintains a separate office or workspace, has business license/tax ID). **All three prongs** must be met for contractor status; failure on any single prong suggests employee status. **Application**: Courts look at the real economic relationship, not contract labels; an agreement stating "independent contractor" does not override the ABC test. **Misclassification consequences**: Classifying an employee as an independent contractor exposes the employer to (1) unemployment-insurance fraud penalties and audit back-assessments, (2) wage-and-hour audits and back-wage claims under La. R.S. 23:631–635 and federal FLSA (including unpaid overtime for prior 3 years), (3) income-tax withholding liability to the IRS and La. Department of Revenue, (4) workers' compensation audit and penalties, (5) civil liability to the worker for breach of wage laws, and (6) potential criminal liability for fraud. The Louisiana Workforce Commission may assess penalties, require reclassification retroactively, and refer cases to the IRS and federal Department of Labor. Written contractor agreements should explicitly document that all three ABC prongs are met and include disclaimers: no benefits, no workers' compensation, contractor provides own tools/equipment, contractor sets own schedule, contractor works for multiple clients.

**Remote Work and Multi-State Employees**: No Louisiana-specific rules govern remote workers or multi-state employees. Federal FLSA applies to wages; Louisiana income-tax withholding is required if the employee is working in Louisiana (verify with Louisiana Department of Revenue). Multi-state employers must comply with all applicable state laws in states where work is performed.

**Reciprocity**: Louisiana recognizes only Louisiana law for employment disputes involving Louisiana-based work. Out-of-state employment contracts may invoke choice-of-law provisions ("This agreement is governed by Florida law"), but if work is performed in Louisiana, **Louisiana law applies** to statutory protections (wages, discrimination, breach notification, workers' compensation, retaliation, etc.). Employers cannot contractually waive or avoid Louisiana law for Louisiana-based work. Employers with multi-state workforces must comply with wage-and-hour, discrimination, and leave laws in all states where employees work. For remote workers based in Louisiana who work partly in other states, Louisiana law applies to the Louisiana-worked portion; multi-state withholding and compliance may be required.

### Workers' compensation retaliation (La. R.S. 23:1361; supplement to section 9)

**Workers' Compensation Retaliation (La. R.S. 23:1361)**: Louisiana law explicitly prohibits employers from retaliating against employees for filing a workers' compensation claim. Retaliation includes: (1) discharging the employee, (2) demoting or suspending the employee, (3) reducing pay or benefits, (4) harassing or intimidating the employee, (5) refusing to employ or rehire the employee because of a prior workers' compensation claim, or (6) any adverse employment action that would discourage filing a claim. Retaliation is prohibited even if the workers' compensation claim was filed in another state or under federal law (such as the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act). **Penalties**: An employer found liable for discrimination against an employee who filed a workers' compensation claim is liable for damages equal to **the amount the employee would have earned but for the discrimination**, based on the starting salary of the position sought or the earnings at the time of discharge, not to exceed one year's earnings, plus **reasonable attorney's fees and court costs**. **Protected period**: The 90-day presumption of retaliation applies; if an adverse action occurs within 90 days of a workers' compensation claim, retaliation is presumed unless the employer provides clear evidence of a legitimate, documented reason. **Reporting**: Employees may file a wrongful-discharge lawsuit in state court within one year (Louisiana's prescriptive period / statute of limitations) or file an administrative complaint with the Louisiana Workforce Commission.

## 11. Implications for Human Capital Management Suite (P1A/P1B)

When the platform processes a promotion, base-pay change, manager change, or termination for a worker based in Louisiana:

**For Promotions and Base-Pay Changes:**

- **Document the effective date and new compensation** in the employee record
- **Record at-will status** in the worker profile (no contract-term field required for most Louisiana workers unless an exception applies)
- **Ensure final-pay calculation complies with La. R.S. 23:631**: Triggered when a termination occurs, not at promotion/pay change, but prepare the system to calculate within 15 days or the next regular payday
- **Track accrued paid time off (if applicable)** and whether it is owed at termination under the employer's policy
- **Document pay frequency** and ensure the promotion/pay-change effective date aligns with the next pay cycle (no specific state requirement, but best practice)
- **Retain all wage and promotion records** for at least 3 years (federal FLSA); recommend 4 years for audit trail
- **Flag noncompete enforceability** if the employer uses noncompete agreements: verify 2-year cap, reasonableness, and physician-specific amendments if applicable

**For Terminations:**

- **Calculate final pay** (all earned wages including overtime through last day, plus accrued leave if applicable) within 15 days of termination or on the next regular payday (La. R.S. 23:631)
- **Check for La. R.S. 23:632 violation risks** and flag if the employer may be in breach
- **File Unemployment Insurance Separation Notice** with LWC (administrative requirement)
- **Implement WARN Act workflows** if employer has 100+ employees and termination affects 50+ workers in 30 days
- **No paid-leave mandate** for private-sector employees, but preserve any accrued leave owed under the employment contract or policy

**For Discrimination and Protected Activity:**

- **Implement workflows** that flag terminations or pay reductions based on protected class (La. R.S. 23:301 et seq.: race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age 40+, disability, pregnancy, genetic information)
- **Age discrimination alerts**: Flag if an employee 40+ is terminated shortly after a performance review mentioning age-related language ("overqualified," "new generation," "native digital") or if compensation is reduced for age-related reasons
- **Pregnancy/medical accommodation tracking**: Document reasonable-accommodation requests (light duty, leave, schedule flexibility); ensure accommodations are provided and documented in the employee record
- **Track retaliation risks** (e.g., termination within 90 days of jury duty, voting, workers' compensation claim, FMLA use, protected complaint, or environmental whistleblower report under La. R.S. 23:967)
- **Protected-activity documentation**: If an employee makes a protected complaint (safety, environmental, wage, discrimination), preserve all related records and communications; destruction may be considered spoliation
- **Complaint procedures**: Maintain a documented process for employees to report discrimination or retaliation; train managers to report complaints to HR and not to retaliate

**Data Security and Breach Notification:**

- **If personal information is exposed** (SSN, driver's license, financial account), trigger notification workflow within "without unreasonable delay" standard (recommend 2–3 days for safety)

**Drug Testing and E-Verify:**

- **If the employer requires drug testing**, ensure procedures are documented and notices are posted (La. R.S. 23:1017.1 et seq.)
- **If the employee works on public contracts or is funded by state money**, ensure E-Verify verification is documented (La. R.S. 42:1451)

**Open Questions for Louisiana Compliance:**

1. **Final Pay and Wage Deductions:**
   - Does the employer have a written policy on final-pay timing (15 days, next payday, or longer)?
   - How are overtime hours calculated for the final paycheck (through the last day worked or through the end of the pay period)?
   - What deductions are permitted in the final check (tax, court-ordered, voluntary)?

2. **Noncompetes and IP:**
   - Does the employer have a noncompete/nonsolicitation policy? If so, are agreements reviewed for La. R.S. 23:921 compliance (2-year cap, reasonable scope, legitimate business interest, physician-specific rules)?
   - Are noncompete agreements signed before or after hire? If after hire, what consideration was provided?
   - How does the employer enforce noncompetes (litigation, cease-and-desist, injunction)?

3. **Employment Status and Contracts:**
   - Does any employee have a written employment contract specifying a term (e.g., 1 year, 2 years) or termination notice period?
   - How are independent contractors classified (ABC test: control, outside usual business, independently established)?
   - Are contractor agreements include appropriate disclaimers (no benefits, no workers' comp, at-will)?

4. **Drug Testing and Background Checks:**
   - Does the employer require drug testing? If so, are procedures documented, notices posted, SAMHSA labs used, and positive results confirmed?
   - Are background checks conducted through third-party vendors? If so, is FCRA notice and authorization obtained before screening?
   - Are criminal records inquiries made for all positions or only certain roles?

5. **Leave, PTO, and Benefits:**
   - Does the employer have a paid-leave or PTO policy? If so, are accrued balances treated as earned compensation or discretionary benefits? Are carryover and payout rules clear?
   - If FMLA-eligible, does the employer designate paid leave to run concurrently with FMLA, or does FMLA run separately?
   - Does the employer offer health insurance? If so, is COBRA continuation offered on termination?

6. **Discrimination and Retaliation Protection:**
   - Does the employer have written anti-discrimination and retaliation policies? Are they communicated to all employees?
   - Does the employer track age, pregnancy, disability, medical accommodations, protected complaints? Is all documentation retained for 3+ years?
   - Are performance reviews and termination decisions documented with business reasons unrelated to protected status?

7. **Data and Privacy:**
   - Does the employer maintain unencrypted personal information (SSN, driver's license, financial account numbers, health records)? If so, is a breach-notification procedure in place?
   - Does the employer collect biometric data or conduct electronic monitoring? If so, is notice provided and consent obtained?
   - Does the employer request or review personal social-media passwords or accounts? (Prohibited under La. R.S. 51:1951.)

### Critical compliance checklist

To operationalize this research in Human Capital Management Suite's workflows, prioritize these controls:

1. **At-Will Documentation**: Confirm written at-will employment status on all hires and in employee handbooks, noting public-policy exceptions (retaliation, discrimination, protected activity).
2. **Final Pay Compliance**: Implement final-pay workflows triggering within 15 days of termination or next regular payday (La. R.S. 23:631); calculate all earned wages, accrued overtime, and accrued leave payout (if policy mandates).
3. **No Paid-Leave Mandate**: Communicate that Louisiana has no statewide paid sick or family leave requirement for private employees; local ordinances (e.g., New Orleans) are preempted by La. R.S. 23:642.
4. **Contractor Classification**: Audit all contractors against La. R.S. 23:1471 ABC test; document that all three prongs are met; flag misclassifications for reclassification remediation.
5. **Discrimination and Retaliation Protections**: Flag terminations/pay changes for protected classes (age 40+, race, sex, disability, pregnancy, religious accommodation, political activity); implement retaliation prevention for jury duty, voting, workers' compensation, FMLA, and whistleblower activity.
6. **Noncompete Enforceability**: Ensure all noncompete agreements comply with La. R.S. 23:921 2-year cap, reasonable scope/territory, and legitimate business interest; document enforceability foundations before separation.
7. **Breach Notification Response**: Maintain breach-notification procedures; notify affected individuals within 2-3 business days per La. R.S. 51:3071 ("without unreasonable delay").
8. **Drug Testing Procedures**: If testing is required, use SAMHSA-certified labs, confirm positive results, provide notice, follow chain of custody, and document procedures (La. R.S. 23:1017.1 et seq.).
9. **E-Verify Compliance** (if applicable): For public-contract or state-funded employees, ensure E-Verify verification (La. R.S. 42:1451, R.S. 38:2212.10); private employers are not required but may use E-Verify voluntarily.
10. **Data and Privacy**: Ensure personal information is encrypted, access controls are in place, and social-media password requests are prohibited (La. R.S. 51:1951); implement privacy policies in employee handbooks.
