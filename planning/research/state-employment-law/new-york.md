# New York Employment Law Research

**State:** New York | **Researched:** 2026-09-03 | **Status:** REVIEWED

## 1. Summary for HCM Next

- **At-will employment with strong statutory protections**: New York recognizes at-will termination but prohibits discharge for whistleblowing, jury duty, wage claims, military service, workers' compensation claims, and public-policy violations; violations trigger damages and attorney fees.
- **Wage Theft Prevention Act (Labor Law § 195)**: Mandatory written notice at hire and 7 days before any pay-rate decrease; notice of pay statements at each payday. Violations subject to 6-year statute of limitations, 200% liquidated damages, attorney fees, and civil penalties (§ 198).
- **Regional minimum wage tiers (Labor Law § 652, effective 2026)**: NYC/Long Island/Westchester $17.00/hour; rest of state $16.00/hour; frequent annual increases by CPI-W.
- **Pay Transparency (§ 194-b, effective Sept 17, 2023)**: Employers with 4+ employees must include good-faith wage ranges and job descriptions in all postings, transfers, and promotions. Internal promotions also require wage-range disclosure.
- **Salary history ban (§ 194-a)**: Cannot inquire about prior wages; retaliation for refusal prohibited.
- **Equal Pay (§ 194)**: Unlawful to pay protected-class employees at lower rates for substantially similar work under comparable conditions; covers 21 protected classes including race, sex, age, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, ancestry, creed, national origin.
- **Paid sick leave (§ 196-b)**: 1 hour per 30 hours worked (40 hours/year max); accrues on first day; job-protected for health, family care, domestic violence, school needs, public health emergencies.
- **Paid Family Leave (Workers' Comp Law Art. 9)**: 12 weeks at 67% of average weekly wage for bonding with newborn or caring for ill family member. Paid Prenatal Leave (§ 196-c, effective Jan 1, 2025): 20 hours in 52-week period for prenatal appointments.
- **NY WARN Act (Labor Law Art. 25-A)**: Stricter than federal—50+ employees (not 100), 90 days notice (not 60), triggered by 25+ layoffs in 30 days. Penalties: $10,000/day per violation plus severance.
- **Ban-the-box (Executive Law § 296(16), 4+ employees)**: Cannot inquire about criminal record during initial application phase unless law requires or position demands background check.
- **Automated employment decision tools (NYC Local Law 144, effective Jan 1, 2023)**: Employers using AEDTs for hiring/promotion must commission independent annual bias audit, post summary, and provide 10 business days' notice to candidates before tool use.
- **Electronic monitoring notice (Civil Rights Law § 201-i, effective May 7, 2022)**: Written notice required to new hires and posted in workplace if employer monitors email, telephone, or internet; penalties $500–$3,000 per violation.
- **Reproductive health nondiscrimination (§ 203-e)**: Cannot discriminate or take adverse action based on reproductive health decisions; employee handbook notice required as of Jan 2, 2025.

## 2. Employment Relationship: At-Will Status and Exceptions

New York follows at-will employment doctrine but recognizes significant statutory and common-law exceptions.

**Exceptions to at-will termination**:

- Termination for filing wage theft, workers' compensation, or unemployment benefit claims (Labor Law § 740 as amended 2022).
- Termination for exercising constitutional rights (free speech, assembly, religious observance, jury duty, voting, military service).
- Termination in violation of public policy (whistleblowing, refusing to commit illegal act, reporting safety violations).
- Termination based on past criminal conviction without compliance with Correction Law Art. 23-A (Rosario factors).
- Labor Law § 215: Retaliation prohibited for reporting violations or filing complaints with NY Department of Labor; 6-year statute of limitations.

**Required notices at hire and on change**:

- Labor Law § 195(1) requires written notice at hire containing: rate of pay, frequency of pay, allowances claimed, deductions, schedule, and employer name and address. Notice must be provided in language employee understands or can readily obtain translation.
- § 195(2): Employers must provide written notice of wage-rate changes at least 7 days before effective date, unless increase can be announced on pay stub (increases need not precede the pay period).
- § 195(3) requires pay statement (stub) with each payment showing gross pay, net pay, deductions, pay period, and itemization.
- § 195(4) requires records retention for 6 years covering hours, rates, deductions, and final pay.

## 3. Wages: Minimum Wage, Overtime, Pay Frequency, Final Pay, Deductions

**Minimum wage (Labor Law § 652, 12 NYCRR 142)**:

- Effective Jan 1, 2026: $17.00/hour (NYC, Long Island, Westchester); $16.00/hour (rest of state).
- Effective Jan 1, 2025: $16.50/hour (downstate); $15.50/hour (upstate).
- Annual adjustment by CPI-W beginning 2027; increases tied to Consumer Price Index for Northeast Region.
- Tipped employees: $15.05/hour (2026, downstate); $14.10/hour (upstate); employer must supplement if tips do not reach minimum wage.

**Overtime (Labor Law § 191)**:

- Time-and-a-half (1.5x) for hours over 40 per week.
- No daily overtime (8/day) requirement; federal FLSA standard applies.
- Exemptions: agricultural employees, certain executive/administrative/professional employees under federal FLSA definitions.

**Pay frequency and timing (§ 191, § 195)**:

- Manual workers: must be paid weekly.
- Clerical and other workers: per employment contract, not less frequently than semi-monthly (every 2 weeks).
- Designated paydays established in advance by employer.
- If payday falls on non-work day, payment due on preceding work day.
- End of pay period no more than 10 working days before payday.

**Final pay on termination (§ 195)**:

- All wages due must be paid by next regular payday following termination (voluntary resignation or involuntary discharge).
- Includes accrued earned sick leave payout unless employer had written policy limiting payout (policy must have been provided at hire).
- Failure to pay final wages triggers liquidated damages of 200% of unpaid amount, attorney fees, and civil penalties.

**Permitted deductions (§ 193, § 198)**:

- Only deductions required by law (tax, garnishment, court order) or authorized in writing by employee for purposes permitted by law.
- Employers cannot require employees to bear cost of uniforms, tools, or business expenses if deduction would reduce wages below minimum wage.
- Prohibited deductions: uniforms, tools, equipment, breakage, shortages absent voluntary agreement and no reduction below minimum.
- Violations prosecuted as wage theft; 6-year statute of limitations under Wage Theft Prevention Act § 198.

**Pay statement content**:

- § 195(3) requires itemized wage statements showing: gross pay, net pay, rate of pay, pay period, deductions with reason and amount, employer name and address.
- Electronic pay stubs permitted if accessible to employee; print-on-demand required if requested.

## 4. Pay Transparency and Equity

**Wage range disclosure (Labor Law § 194-b, effective September 17, 2023)**:

- Employers with 4 or more employees must include wage ranges (minimum and maximum or minimum and midpoint) and job descriptions in all job postings, promotion notices, and transfer opportunities.
- Ranges must reflect good-faith compensation intended for position at time of posting; artificially broad ranges designed to circumvent law are prohibited.
- Applies to positions involving work in New York or reporting to supervisor/office in New York, including remote positions.
- Covers all posting formats: websites, social media, print, email, newsletters, internal postings.
- NYC Local Law 32 (2022) extends requirement within NYC with enforcement by Department of Consumer and Worker Protection.
- Failure to post ranges enforceable through administrative complaint and civil action; employers subject to liquidated damages and attorney fees.

**Salary history ban (§ 194-a, effective January 1, 2020)**:

- Employers cannot ask applicants about wage history, nor can they direct third parties to inquire.
- Candidates may voluntarily disclose prior wages without employer prompting.
- Retaliation (refusing to interview, hire, or employ for refusal to disclose) prohibited; violations trigger administrative enforcement and potential damages.

**Equal pay statute (§ 194)**:

- Unlawful to pay employees of a protected class at lower rate than others for substantially similar work under comparable conditions.
- 21 protected classes: race, color, national origin, ancestry, creed, sex, age (40+), marital status, military status, domestic-violence victim status, disability, gender identity/expression, sexual orientation, genetic information, atypical hereditary cellular/blood trait, predisposing genetic characteristics, carrier status of genetic disorder.
- Remedy includes back pay, front pay, compensatory damages, treble damages if jury finds violation, and attorney fees.
- 6-year statute of limitations applies (Wage Theft Prevention Act extended limitations).

**Pay data reporting**:

- No state-mandated pay-equity audit or EEO reporting requirement; federal EEO-1 applies if 100+ employees.

## 5. Leave and Time: Paid Sick Leave, Family Leave, Interaction with Pay/Role Changes

**Earned Sick and Safe Leave (§ 196-b, effective Sept 30, 2020)**:

- All private employers must provide paid leave: minimum 1 hour per 30 hours worked, up to 40 hours per calendar year.
- Accrual begins on first day of employment; employers may establish benefit-year timing (calendar year, anniversary, or other).
- Permitted uses: diagnosis/care/recovery for employee's own or family member's physical/mental health; care for family member; domestic violence, sexual assault, stalking; child's school conference or activity; workplace/school closure due to public health emergency; quarantine ordered during public health emergency.
- No carryover cap specified; employer may require use-it-or-lose-it policy at year-end or cap carryover at 40 hours (verify current administrative guidance).
- Employee may use leave starting on accrual date; employer may require 7-day notice for foreseeable needs.
- Job-protected; employer cannot retaliate for use.
- If employee leaves employment, accrued but unused leave must be paid out at regular rate unless employer provided written accrual-limit or forfeiture policy at time of hire.

**Paid Prenatal Leave (§ 196-b, effective January 1, 2025)**:

- Employees entitled to up to 20 hours of paid leave in any 52-week period to attend prenatal medical appointments, procedures, testing, fertility treatment, or end-of-pregnancy care.
- Paid at employee's regular rate; may be used in 1-hour increments.
- Separate from earned sick leave and paid family leave.

**Paid Family Leave (Workers' Comp Law Art. 9)**:

- Covers bonding with newborn, adopted child, or foster child; caring for spouse, child, or parent with serious health condition; qualifying exigency due to military member's service.
- Duration: up to 12 weeks per calendar year at 67% of average weekly wage (state-administered insurance program).
- Employer must permit leave; not responsible for wage replacement (state fund pays).
- Job-protected; employer cannot retaliate or terminate due to family leave.

**Interaction with pay/role changes**:

- On promotion during active leave, employee generally assumes promoted role and pay rate upon return.
- Accrued sick leave carries forward to promoted role; employer cannot forfeit accrued balance.
- Leave benefits integrate with pay-change notices; no special timing required beyond standard § 195(2) 7-day notice for pay-rate changes.
- If employee on unpaid leave (e.g., personal LOA), accrual may pause depending on policy (verify case law on integration).

## 6. Records and Access: Retention, Employee Access, Format

**Payroll and personnel record retention (§ 195(4), § 740)**:

- Wage and hour records: employers must retain for 6 years documenting hours worked, rates, deductions, gross/net pay per employee (amended 2025 to expand Wage Theft Prevention Act audit trail).
- Timekeeping records: 6 years minimum.
- Medical/disability records: retain separately for at least 3 years post-termination (ADA/HIPAA requirements apply).
- Personnel files: no comprehensive state-mandated retention schedule; common practice 3 years post-termination; disputed items require employee's written statement affixed to record.

**Employee access to personnel files — corrected 2026-09-03 (DISPUTED: legislative status)**:

- **No general state statute currently mandates employee access to personnel files.** The earlier citation to "pending legislation S.2154" was the wrong bill number; the actual bill is **S.3460 / A.2107** (modeled on Massachusetts's personnel-record law), which would add new Labor Law § 210-b requiring employers to provide a copy of the personnel record within 5 business days of a written request (at no cost), notify employees when negative information is added to the file, retain personnel records for at least 3 years post-termination, and bar retaliation for exercising these rights.
- **DISPUTED / status as of last verification**: the Senate passed S.3460 on 2026-04-22 (38-21) and the Assembly passed A.2107 on 2026-05-19 (92-49); as of the most recent sources found (through roughly 2026-07), the bill was on the Governor's desk awaiting signature and had **not yet been confirmed signed**. Do not treat § 210-b as current law until enactment is confirmed; if signed, employers get 60 days from enactment to comply.
- Under current (pre-enactment) law, employees may request copies of payroll records and wage statements under § 195(4); employers must retain those for dispute resolution. This is narrower than a full personnel-file right.
- Employers may charge reasonable copy fees.

**Record format**:

- Electronic records acceptable if retention and access requirements met; employers may require electronic submission of corrective statements by employees.
- No state mandate for paper vs. electronic; employers responsible for ensuring accessibility and preservation.

## 7. Privacy and Data: Employee Data Privacy, Breach Notification, Biometric/Monitoring, Social Media

**Data breach notification (NYS Information Security Breach and Notification Act, effective March 21, 2025)**:

- Applies to any breach of personal information (name + financial account, SSN, health/medical info, biometric records, etc.) by unauthorized person.
- Notification required to affected New York residents without unreasonable delay (industry standard: within 30 days of discovery).
- Methods: written notice, email, phone, or substitute notice if cost exceeds $250,000 and major media notification is used.
- Notify: NY Attorney General (if 500+ residents affected), NY Department of Financial Services, State Police.
- Civil penalties: up to $20 per instance, up to $250,000 total for notification failures; additional $5,000 per violation if inadequate safeguards.
- Exception: Inadvertent disclosure by authorized employee does not trigger notification if no reasonably foreseeable risk of misuse.

**Electronic monitoring (Civil Rights Law § 201-i, effective May 7, 2022)**:

- Employers who monitor or intercept telephone conversations, emails, or internet access/usage must provide written notice to employees (new hires and current employees) and obtain acknowledgment.
- Notice must be in writing (hard copy or email) and posted in conspicuous workplace location.
- Covers electronic eavesdropping and communications monitoring.
- Penalties for non-compliance: $500 (first violation), $1,000 (second), $3,000 (each subsequent); enforced by NY Attorney General.

**Social media and personal accounts**:

- Employers cannot require applicants or employees to disclose personal social media passwords, usernames, or account access (no state statute; federal guidance applies).
- Employers may monitor publicly available social media but must avoid discrimination claims.

**Data residency**:

- No New York state statute mandating employee data residency; employers may process/store in-state or out-of-state provided federal and state privacy/security laws met (CCPA/CPRA applies to CA residents' data; no state equivalent for NY private employees at this time—verify pending legislation).

**Biometric data**:

- No state-specific statute regulating employee biometric collection (iris, fingerprint, voiceprint); employers should follow federal guidance and reasonable privacy expectations.

## 8. Hiring and Background: Ban-the-Box, Background Checks, Drug Testing, E-Verify

**Ban-the-box (Executive Law § 296(16), effective January 1, 2020, 4+ employees)**:

- Employers cannot make any written or verbal inquiry about applicant's criminal record during initial employment application phase (IEAP).
- Exceptions: position specifically required or permitted by law to conduct criminal background check (law enforcement, corrections, judiciary, emergency management, certain regulated professions); arrests/convictions legally preclude position.
- After first interview, employers may inquire about conviction if related to position duties and under Correction Law Art. 23-A framework.
- Violations: up to $300 per applicant per first violation, escalating penalties for repeat offenses.
- NYC Fair Chance Act (Local Law 73 of 2015) provides similar protections within city; statewide law now supersedes.

**Background checks**:

- No state statute restricting private-sector background checks; federal Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) applies. Employers must provide FCRA notice, dispute rights, and adverse-action procedures before using third-party consumer reports.
- Pre-adverse action disclosure required if report will be basis for rejection.

**Drug testing**:

- No state-level statute banning or regulating private-sector drug testing; federal law applies (pre-employment, reasonable suspicion, post-accident).
- Cannabis: Employers cannot take adverse action based solely on detection of cannabis metabolites from lawful off-duty use (Executive Law § 201-d, as amended); may test for on-the-job impairment or reasonable suspicion.

**E-Verify mandate**:

- No state-specific mandate; federal I-9/E-Verify requirements apply.

**Fingerprinting and SSN (§ 201-a, § 203-d)**:

- § 201-a limits employer fingerprinting to positions where law requires or applicant voluntarily consents; can be condition of employment if background check legally required.
- § 203-d restricts SSN requests; cannot require SSN absent law or necessary employment purpose; cannot use SSN as identifier except where law requires.

## 9. Separation: Mini-WARN, Severance, Notice Requirements, Non-Compete/Non-Solicit Enforceability

**Mass layoff notice (New York WARN Act, Labor Law Art. 25-A)**:

- Applies to employers with 50 or more employees (federal WARN: 100+).
- Requires 90 days' advance written notice (federal WARN: 60 days) before mass layoff, plant closing, or mass termination.
- Triggered by: layoff of 25 or more employees within 30-day period, OR 1/3 of workforce if employer has fewer than 75 employees.
- Notice recipients: all affected employees (including part-time/temporary/contingent), union representatives, NY Department of Labor, local elected officials.
- Mandatory severance: 1 week of pay per year of service for all affected employees (no statutory cap; federal WARN has no severance mandate).
- Additional severance: 4 additional weeks if employer fails to provide 90-day notice.
- Penalties: $10,000 per day per violation; separate civil action available for employee damages.
- Statute of limitations: 6 years (Wage Theft Prevention Act extended).

**Severance**:

- No state-mandated severance obligation absent WARN Act or employment contract.

**Non-compete enforceability**:

- Governed by common law standard articulated in BDO Seidman v. Hirshberg, 93 N.Y.2d 382 (1999): non-compete enforceable if (1) protects legitimate employer interests (trade secrets, confidential info, customer relationships, goodwill), (2) imposes no undue hardship on employee, (3) does not injure public.
- Reasonable in duration (typically 6 months to 2 years depending on industry), geography, and scope of restricted activities.
- Courts apply "blue pencil" doctrine permitting judicial narrowing of overly broad agreements.
- Pending legislation S.4641 (2025) would ban non-competes except for employees earning $250,000+ annually (threshold adjusted annually by CPI); status unclear for 2026 session (verify current legislative status).
- A.1361 (2025) would codify BDO Seidman standard and protect employees dismissed without "good cause" from post-employment restrictions.

**Non-solicitation enforceability**:

- No specific statute; enforced under common law reasonableness standard (similar to non-competes).

## 10. Classification and Multi-State: Contractor Tests, Remote Workers, Reciprocity

**Independent contractor classification**:

- No ABC test codified in New York employment law (AB-5 model applies in some states).
- Common law "control test" applies: individual classified as independent contractor if employer does not control manner/means of work performance.
- Focus on: right to control, provision of tools/equipment, method of payment, opportunity for profit/loss, integration into business operations.
- Misclassification triggers wage and hour liability, payroll taxes, and workers' compensation insurance obligations.

**Multi-state employment**:

- No state reciprocity doctrine. If employee works across state lines, each state's laws apply to work performed in that state.
- HCM platform should flag multi-state workers and trigger compliance checks for all applicable jurisdictions.

**Remote workers**:

- If employee works in New York (even remotely for out-of-state employer), New York employment law applies to that work.
- Executive Law § 296 (Human Rights Law) applies to discrimination claims by remote NY workers.
- Wage and hour laws (§ 191, § 195, § 652) apply to work performed in NY.
- Multi-state remote worker: apply law of state where work is performed; verify employer's nexus to each state.

## 11. Implications for P1A/P1B (HCM Platform Modeling: Promotion + Base-Pay Change)

**Mandatory obligations and field restrictions for promotion + base-pay change**:

1. **Pay Transparency Disclosure (§ 194-b, § 195(2))**: On creation of promotion posting (internal or external), require wage-range input (minimum and maximum at time of posting). Block posting if range field empty. Timestamp posting date for compliance audit. For internal promotion to 4+ employee organization, include wage range in promotion notice and track disclosure timing.

2. **Pay Rate Change Notice (§ 195(2))**: If promotion involves pay decrease, generate mandatory 7-day advance written notice with new rate, frequency, and effective date. System default: permits pay increases without advance notice (can be shown on pay stub). Store all notices for 6-year audit trail (§ 195(4)).

3. **Wage Theft Prevention Act Compliance (§ 195(1), § 198)**: Log all pay-rate changes and link to written notice delivery. Non-compliance triggers 6-year retaliation/wage-theft liability with 200% liquidated damages and attorney fees. Automated escalation if 7-day notice window violated.

4. **Equal Pay Audit Flag (§ 194)**: On promotion + base-pay change, system should identify peer employees performing "substantially similar work" in same geographic area (county minimum, employer scope optimal) and flag if new rate differs >15% without documented justification (skill differential, seniority, merit system). Require manager/HR sign-off to override flag, with reason field logged.

5. **Accrued Paid Sick Leave Handling (§ 196-b)**: Track accrued sick leave (1 hour per 30 hours worked, 40-hour annual max). On promotion, carry accrued balance forward; do not forfeit. On termination, pay out accrued balance at regular rate unless written accrual-limit policy was provided at hire (system should flag if no policy exists).

6. **Paid Family Leave Integration (Workers' Comp Law Art. 9, § 196-c)**: On promotion during active family leave or prenatal leave, ensure employee returns to promoted role and updated pay rate. No employer contributions required (state-funded); log role change in leave-management system.

7. **WARN Act Trigger (Labor Law Art. 25-A)**: If promotion/pay change is part of workforce restructuring affecting 25+ employees and employer has 50+ total employees, auto-flag and require 90-day mass-layoff notice scheduling (not 60-day federal standard). Calculate mandatory severance (1 week/year of service) and 4-week penalty if notice inadequate.

8. **Ban-the-Box Compliance on Role Change (§ 296(16))**: If promotion posting created for open position, do not request criminal history on initial application. If position legally requires background check, defer criminal inquiry until after first interview and comply with Correction Law Art. 23-A (Rosario factors).

9. **Automated Employment Decision Tool Notice (NYC Local Law 144)**: If HCM Next uses algorithm or ML model to rank promotion candidates, conduct independent annual bias audit, post summary on employer website, and provide 10 business days' notice to candidates before AEDT evaluation. Non-compliance: $500–$1,500/day penalties.

10. **Reproductive Health and Electronic Monitoring Notices**: Ensure employee handbook includes § 203-e (reproductive health nondiscrimination) and § 201-i (electronic monitoring) notices; update as of January 2, 2025.

11. **Multi-State Worker Flagging**: If promoted employee works across state lines, system should identify and trigger compliance review for all applicable states (wage ranges, pay-change notice timing, leave interaction, tax withholding).

**Open questions for legal review**:

- Verify whether "substantially similar work" for equal-pay audit under § 194 includes geographic limits (same county, region, or employer-wide scope).
- Clarify calculation of "comparable conditions" standard in equal-pay cases (same department, supervisor, shift, or broader composite).
- Confirm automated decision tool threshold: does every algorithmic promotion ranking trigger NYC Local Law 144, or only machine-learning models?
- Verify current legislative status of S.4641 (non-compete ban) and A.1361 (codification + good-cause protection) for 2026.
- Confirm whether accrued sick leave continues to accrue during unpaid family leave or personal LOA.
- Verify whether paid family leave payout is required on termination or only state-administered benefit access.
