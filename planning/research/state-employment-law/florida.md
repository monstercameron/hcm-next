# Florida Employment Law Research

**Jurisdiction:** Florida (FL)  
**Researched:** 2026-09-03  
**Status:** DRAFTED (unreviewed)

## 1. Summary for HCM Next

- **Minimum wage (effective Sept 30, 2025):** $14.00/hr general, $10.98/hr tipped employees; escalates to $15.00 on Sept 30, 2026, then annual CPI adjustments thereafter (Fla. Const. Art. X § 24).
- **At-will employment:** Default rule under common law; exceptions: whistleblower retaliation (Fla. Stat. § 448.101–105), jury duty, unlawful discrimination, workers' compensation retaliation (Fla. Stat. § 440.205).
- **Overtime:** Governed by federal FLSA; Florida has no independent overtime law. Non-exempt employees earn 1.5x minimum wage ($21/hr as of Sept 2025) for hours over 40/week.
- **Pay frequency:** No state mandate; employers may use weekly, bi-weekly, semi-monthly, or monthly schedules. Must establish regular payday and adhere to it.
- **Final pay on termination:** FLSA applies; pay earned on next regular payday or within 72 hours if 72+ hours' notice given (federal requirements under FLSA override if more restrictive).
- **Non-compete and non-solicit:** Enforceable under Fla. Stat. § 542.335 if in writing, protect a legitimate business interest, and reasonable in time/geography/scope. CHOICE Act (eff. July 1, 2025) strengthened enforcement for non-competes but left non-solicits unchanged.
- **Severance:** No state mandate; voluntary unless specified in contract or policy.
- **E-Verify:** Mandatory for private employers with 25+ employees (SB 1718, eff. July 1, 2023); applies to newly hired employees only.
- **No paid leave mandate:** Florida has no state requirement for paid sick leave, paid family leave, or vacation payout on termination.
- **Data breach notification:** FIPA (Fla. Stat. § 501.171) requires notification to affected individuals within 30 days of breach discovery.

## 2. Employment Relationship: At-Will Status and Exceptions

Florida is an at-will employment state. Both employer and employee may terminate the relationship at any time, for any reason, or for no reason, with or without notice, absent an exception (common law doctrine).

**Exceptions to at-will:**

1. **Whistleblower Protection (Fla. Stat. § 448.101–105):** Private employers may not retaliate against employees who report illegal activity, cooperate with government investigations, or refuse to participate in conduct violating law. **Key requirement:** Employee must first provide written notice to a supervisor or employer and give the employer a reasonable opportunity to remedy the illegal conduct before reporting to a government agency. Failure to provide written internal notice first voids protection.

2. **Jury Duty (Fla. Stat. § 40.271):** Employer cannot discharge or threaten discharge because employee was summoned for or served on jury duty.

3. **Discrimination:** Termination based on unlawful discrimination (race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age, disability, marital status, familial status under Fla. Civil Rights Act; sexual orientation and gender identity in some municipalities) is prohibited.

4. **Workers' Compensation Retaliation (Fla. Stat. § 440.205):** Employer cannot discharge, discriminate against, or retaliate against employee for filing a workers' compensation claim or in connection with a work-related injury.

**No state requirement for notice of separation.** WARN Act (federal; 20 CFR 639) applies if mass layoff or plant closure affects 50+ employees at a site where employer has 100+ employees.

**No written notice required at hire.** Employers may establish pay terms orally or in writing. No mandatory written employment agreement or at-will notice required by state law.

## 3. Wages: Minimum Wage, Overtime, Pay Frequency, Final Pay, Deductions

**Minimum wage (Fla. Const. Art. X § 24):** $14.00/hr as of Sept 30, 2025, for all non-exempt employees. $10.98/hr for tipped employees (employers may credit tips toward minimum wage if tips do not bring employee to $14.00/hr). Escalates to $15.00/hr on Sept 30, 2026; thereafter increases by $1.00/yr until $15.00 reached, then annual CPI adjustment.

**Overtime:** Florida has no separate state overtime law. FLSA applies: non-exempt employees earn minimum 1.5x regular rate for hours over 40/week. At current $14.00 minimum, overtime minimum is $21/hr. Exempt employees include executive, administrative, and professional categories paid at least $684/week salary; highly compensated earning $107,432+/yr.

**Pay frequency:** No state law mandates pay frequency. Employers may use weekly, bi-weekly, semi-monthly, or monthly schedules at their discretion, but must establish a regular payday and adhere to it.

**Final paycheck on termination/resignation:** Governed by FLSA. All wages earned must be paid on the next regular payday. If employee resigns with 72+ hours' notice, final check due within 72 hours of separation; if less than 72 hours' notice, due on next regular payday. Final check must include regular wages, accrued commissions, and bonuses if earned. **Accrued but unused vacation or paid time off is not mandated to be paid out by Florida law** (no vesting requirement), though employers may voluntarily provide this if policy or contract specifies.

**Deductions:** Employers may deduct amounts authorized by federal or state law. Deductions may not reduce wages below minimum wage for hours worked. No statutory restrictions on categories (garnishments, uniforms, tools, breakage, etc.) beyond federal limits and prohibition against reducing below minimum.

**Pay statement (wage stub):** No state mandate for most employers. Exception: Labor pools (Fla. Stat. § 448.24) must provide itemized written wage statement at each pay period showing all deductions, and annual earnings summary by Feb 1. Labor pools must also compensate day laborers at minimum wage with deductions not reducing pay below minimum.

## 4. Pay Transparency and Equity

**Salary range disclosure:** No Florida state law requires disclosure of salary ranges in job postings or upon employee request. Disclosure is voluntary.

**Salary history ban:** No Florida state law prohibits requesting or relying on salary history. Employers may inquire about prior compensation.

**Pay data reporting:** No state-mandated pay equity reporting or data disclosure requirements for private employers.

**Equal pay law:** Fla. Civil Rights Act (Fla. Stat. § 760.10) and federal Equal Pay Act (EPA) both apply. Employers may not discriminate based on sex by paying lower wages for equal work requiring equal skill, effort, responsibility, and performed under similar working conditions. Employers may justify a pay difference by proving a seniority system, merit system, production-based compensation, or a reasonable factor other than sex. Violations of state equal pay can result in up to $10,000 civil penalty per violation plus injunctive relief. Federal EPA remedies apply concurrently.

**Protected classes:** Fla. Civil Rights Act prohibits discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age (40+), disability, marital status, and familial status. Sexual orientation and gender identity protections vary by municipality (Miami, Tampa, Tallahassee, Orlando, Jacksonville, Gainesville, and others have local ordinances); state law does not yet cover these.

## 5. Leave and Time: Paid Leave, Mandated Leave, Interaction with Pay/Role Change

**No paid sick leave mandate:** Florida does not require employers to provide paid sick leave. Employers offering sick leave do so voluntarily.

**No paid family/medical leave mandate:** Florida has no state paid family leave or paid medical leave requirement. Federal FMLA applies to employers with 50+ employees within 75 miles; FMLA provides unpaid, job-protected leave but does not require pay continuation.

**Workers' Compensation leave (Fla. Stat. § 440.102):** Employees with work-connected compensable injuries are carried in full-pay status for up to 40 work hours without being required to use accrued personal leave, available for Workers' Compensation-related appointments.

**Other mandated leave:** No state mandate for military leave, voting leave, or other leave types beyond federal requirements (jury duty, FMLA, military USERRA).

**Interaction with pay/role change:** Changing an employee's pay rate or role does not automatically trigger accrual or payout of leave. Leave balances and policies continue unless explicitly modified by the employer. No "use-it-or-lose-it" prohibition; employers may set reasonable limits on accrual or carryover (verify state common law on notice requirements for policy changes).

## 6. Records and Access: Retention, Personnel File Access, Record Format

**Payroll record retention:** FLSA requires 3 years minimum. Florida best practice: retain payroll records for at least 5 years. Records must include employee name, hours worked, gross and net wages, deductions.

**Personnel records retention:** Best practice is 5 years after employee departure for general employment records (performance reviews, disciplinary action, termination documentation). Medical/health records subject to stricter requirements (30 years under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1020). I-9 forms must be retained for 3 years per federal law.

**Employee access to personnel file:** Private-sector employees have no absolute right to review their personnel files under state law. Florida public records law protects public employees' access to their records, but private employers may decide whether to grant access. Employers may charge a reasonable copying fee if allowing access.

**Record format:** No Florida law mandates electronic vs. paper format. Employers may maintain records in either format, or both, as long as they are complete and accurate.

**Data retention for employment changes:** No state-specific requirement to document notice, acknowledgment, or consent to pay/role changes in writing, but federal wage-and-hour compliance and common-law contract principles recommend written documentation of material terms when changed.

## 7. Privacy and Data: State Privacy Law, Monitoring, Breach Notification

**State employee privacy law:** Florida has no comprehensive employee privacy statute analogous to CCPA. The Florida Information Protection Act (FIPA; Fla. Stat. § 501.171) applies to personal information generally, including employee data.

**Biometric and monitoring:** No state law restricting employee monitoring, surveillance, or biometric collection (e.g., fingerprints, facial recognition). Federal law and industry-specific rules (e.g., healthcare, financial) may apply. Employers should disclose monitoring practices; undisclosed monitoring may trigger common-law torts or privacy claims.

**Data breach notification (FIPA; Fla. Stat. § 501.171):** Businesses must notify every Florida resident whose personal information was or may have been accessed without authorization within **30 days** of discovery of breach. Notification must describe the breach and steps affected individual should take. If breach affects 500+ Florida residents, must also notify Florida Department of Legal Affairs. Civil penalties for FIPA violation: $1,000/day for first 30 days of noncompliance, $50,000 for each subsequent 30-day period. Violations continuing 180+ days incur maximum $500,000 penalty.

**Good faith exception:** Access to personal information by an employee or agent for a legitimate business purpose, without improper use or unauthorized sharing, does not trigger breach notification.

**Employee data processing:** No state requirement for data residency or processing location. Employers may store employee data in cloud systems or third-party services; federal law (e.g., GLBA for financial, HIPAA for health data) may apply to specific data types.

## 8. Hiring and Background: Ban-the-Box, Background Checks, Drug Testing, E-Verify

**Ban-the-box:** No statewide Florida law restricting criminal history inquiries. **Local ordinances:** Miami-Dade County prohibits criminal history questions and background checks until candidate is a finalist. Gainesville prohibits inquiries until conditional offer. Tallahassee, Tampa, and Miami-Dade apply ban-the-box to public employment roles. Employers in covered municipalities must defer criminal history questions.

**Background checks:** No state restrictions on timing, scope, or content of background checks beyond federal FCRA requirements. Employers must disclose intent to conduct pre-employment background check in writing, obtain written consent, and follow adverse action process (notice and opportunity to respond) if declining to hire based on check results. Background checks for certain roles (childcare, elderly care, healthcare, education) are strongly recommended or mandated by specific licensing boards.

**Drug testing:** No state prohibition on pre-employment or workplace drug testing. FCRA disclosures and consent required if third-party testing used. Safety-sensitive positions (transportation, healthcare, etc.) may require testing.

**E-Verify (SB 1718, eff. July 1, 2023):** Mandatory for private employers with 25+ employees (statewide headcount, not Florida-only). Applies to all newly hired employees (excludes independent contractors). Employers must verify employment eligibility using federal E-Verify system by electronically matching I-9 Form against Social Security Administration and Department of Homeland Security records. Must retain I-9 and employment eligibility documents for 3 years. Penalties for non-compliance: up to $1,000/day fine if employer fails E-Verify 3+ times in any 24-month period, with potential suspension/revocation of business licenses, franchises, permits, certifications, registrations, charters, or similar authorization required by law.

## 9. Separation: Mini-WARN Acts, Severance, Notice, Non-Compete/Non-Solicit

**WARN Act (Federal; 20 CFR Part 639):** Applies to Florida employers with 100+ employees. Requires 60 days' written advance notice for mass layoffs (50+ employees at single site) or plant closings (site closure resulting in 50+ job losses). Notice must go to affected workers/union representatives, State Rapid Response Coordinator, and chief elected official of local jurisdiction. Purpose: allow workers time to seek alternative employment or training.

**No state mini-WARN:** Florida has no independent state-level WARN act. Federal WARN is the only requirement.

**Severance:** No state mandate for severance. Severance is voluntary unless specified in contract or company policy. If offered, must be documented in writing with payment amount, timing, conditions.

**Notice of separation:** No state requirement to provide separation notice. WARN Act is the only federal exception for mass layoffs/closures (see above).

**Non-compete (Fla. Stat. § 542.335):** Enforceable if (1) in writing and signed by employee, (2) protects a legitimate business interest (trade secrets, confidential information, substantial customer relationships, customer goodwill associated with trade name/location, extraordinary/specialized training), and (3) is reasonable in duration, geography, and scope of restricted activity.

For employees, agents, and independent contractors: 6 months or less is presumed reasonable; over 2 years is presumed unreasonable. Courts weigh middle-range durations case-by-case.

For distributors, dealers, franchisees, licensees: 1 year or less is presumed reasonable; over 3 years is presumed unreasonable.

**CHOICE Act (eff. July 1, 2025):** Strengthened enforceability of certain non-compete agreements; narrower restrictions (non-solicits, confidentiality) remain under existing framework.

**Non-solicit (Fla. Stat. § 542.335):** Enforceable under same reasonableness standard if designed to protect legitimate business interest in substantial customer relationships. Presumed reasonable if 5 years or less.

## 10. Classification and Multi-State: Contractor Tests, Remote Workers, Reciprocity

**Independent contractor classification:** No state-specific ABC test. Florida courts apply common-law control test: whether employer had right to control manner and means of performance. IRS 20-factor test may also apply for tax purposes. Misclassification can trigger workers' compensation liability, payroll tax liability, and wage-and-hour violations.

**Remote workers:** No state law restricting remote work or imposing different rules for employees working from home vs. office. Federal wage-and-hour laws apply equally to remote and in-office employees. Verify whether employer must withhold Florida income tax (Florida has no state income tax, simplifying calculation).

**Multi-state employment:** If employee works in multiple states, the state where work is performed may impose its own wage, hour, leave, and tax obligations. Employer must comply with each applicable state's requirements. No blanket Florida rule determining which state's law applies; typically the state of work performance governs. **Verify** state-specific multi-state guidance for your HCM Next connector; multi-state rules vary widely.

**Reciprocity:** No Florida statute on interstate employment reciprocity. Federal law applies (e.g., FLSA, Title VII). Multi-state employers must model each state's rules and trigger appropriate notifications/restrictions based on employee location.

## 11. Implications for P1A/P1B

**Promotion:**

- Update pay rate and role: Verify no non-compete or non-solicit restriction triggered by role change. No state law requires written notice of promotion, but best practice is to document. Update payroll system with new title and salary.
- No paid leave resets on promotion; leave balances continue unless policy specifies otherwise. Verify internal policy on any special promotion bonuses or equity vesting.
- No severance triggered. Document promotion in personnel file.

**Base-pay increase:**

- No state requirement for advance notice of pay raise. Update payroll by next regular payday.
- FLSA overtime thresholds: If employee was exempt at prior salary, verify still exempt at new rate (minimum $684/week salary basis). If raising to make newly exempt, ensure duties still meet exemption criteria.
- If using E-Verify employer, no new verification required for existing employee.
- No tax withholding changes required by state (no state income tax in Florida).

**Manager change (reassignment without role change):**

- No legal trigger for written notice, pay adjustment, or leave reset.
- Verify non-solicit and non-compete remain valid (reporting-line change does not typically invalidate restrictive covenants, but verify).
- Update records; no notification requirement.

**Termination:**

- Final paycheck: Must pay all earned wages on next regular payday (or within 72 hours if 72+ hours' notice of resignation given). Include accrued commissions, bonuses if earned; do not require payout of unused vacation unless policy or contract specifies.
- WARN Act: If employer has 100+ employees and termination is part of mass layoff/plant closure (50+ jobs), provide 60 days' notice to workers, state, and local government.
- Non-compete/non-solicit: Restrictions survive termination. If employee is breaching, remedy via injunctive action (cannot force payment of damages as liquidated damages without proving legitimate business interest and reasonableness).
- No severance mandate. If offered, document in separation agreement.
- Data: Retain personnel file, payroll records, I-9, and employment eligibility documents per retention schedule (3–5 years typical; medical records 30 years).
