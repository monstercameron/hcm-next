# Mississippi Employment Law Research

**State:** Mississippi | **Researched:** 2026-09-03 | **Status:** REVIEWED

## 1. Summary for HCM Next

- **At-will employment prevails**: Mississippi recognizes at-will employment by default; either party may terminate for any reason, any time, subject to federal discrimination protections and public policy exceptions.
- **No state minimum wage**: Federal FLSA minimum ($7.25/hour) applies; Mississippi's statutory minimum ($5.15/hour) is preempted.
- **E-Verify mandatory for ALL employers**: Miss. Code § 71-11-3 (Mississippi Employment Protection Act) requires every employer, regardless of size, to verify work eligibility via E-Verify; this is the strictest state requirement.
- **Final pay on next regular payday**: No state-specific deadline; federal FLSA applies; most private employers have discretion on pay schedule (weekly, biweekly, monthly) except manufacturers and public service employers (50+ employees must pay bi-weekly or semi-monthly per Miss. Code § 71-1-35).
- **Equal pay for equal work (sex only)**: Miss. Code § 71-17-5 (effective 2022) prohibits sex-based wage discrimination for "equal work" (requires equal skill, effort, education, responsibility); applies to employers with 5+ employees; private lawsuit enforcement only.
- **No state anti-discrimination law for private employers**: Title VII (federal) applies; no state agency enforces anti-discrimination; coverage gap for employers with < 15 employees.
- **No mandated paid leave**: Federal FMLA (12 weeks unpaid for 50+ employers within 75 miles) applies; no state paid sick leave or paid family leave for private sector (state employees only).
- **3-year payroll records retention**: Federal FLSA requires 3-year retention; no state-specific mandate supersedes it.
- **Data breach notification without unreasonable delay**: Miss. Code § 75-24-29 requires notice in "most expedient time possible and without unreasonable delay"; no specific day count; no private right of action.
- **Noncompetes enforceable if reasonable**: Enforceability determined by reasonableness of scope, duration, geographic area under case law; Mississippi courts scrutinize closely; burden on employer to prove reasonableness.

## 2. Employment Relationship: At-Will Status and Written Notices

Mississippi is an at-will employment state. Under common law and statute, either party may terminate the relationship at any time, for any reason, or for no reason, **except** termination cannot be based on illegal discrimination (federal Title VII, ADA, ADEA, Equal Pay Act) or retaliation for reporting illegal acts (public policy exception; common law tort action available).

No Mississippi statute requires written notice to the employee at hire regarding at-will status, wage theft prevention, or pay-rate changes. Federal FLSA guidance applies. Employers may document at-will agreements and pay-rate changes for internal compliance; no state-specific form or timing is mandated.

Employment contracts are permitted and enforceable if they specify term or conditions. Employee handbooks may create enforceable obligations if they promise specific procedures for discipline or termination.

## 3. Wages: Minimum, Overtime, Pay Frequency, Final Pay, and Deductions

**Minimum Wage**: Federal FLSA floor ($7.25/hour) applies. Miss. Code § 71-1-51 establishes no state minimum; federal law governs.

**Overtime**: Federal FLSA rules apply. Overtime is 1.5× regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. Executive, administrative, professional, and outside-sales exemptions follow FLSA definitions (29 U.S.C. § 213).

**Pay Frequency**: Employers have discretion to set pay schedules (weekly, biweekly, monthly, etc.) for most workers. **Exception**: Miss. Code § 71-1-35 requires manufacturers and public service corporations with 50+ employees to pay wages bi-weekly or semi-monthly, or on the second and fourth Saturdays of each month. Payment must include all wages earned within the preceding 10 days (15 days for public service corporations). Tipped minimum is $2.13/hour; if tips do not bring total to $7.25/hour, employer must make up the difference.

**Final Paycheck on Termination or Resignation**: No state-specific timing mandate. Federal FLSA requires all earned wages be paid in full, typically by the next regular payday. No state law mandates severance; it is a matter of contract.

**Deductions and Pay Statements**: Federal FLSA governs permissible deductions (only deductions required by law, court order, or authorized in writing by employee are allowed). Mississippi employers are required to provide an itemized statement of deductions for each pay period, though no formal pay-stub statute exists. Federal FLSA record-keeping applies.

## 4. Pay Transparency and Equity

**Salary History Bans**: No Mississippi state law restricts inquiries about salary history. Employers may ask about and rely upon prior wage information.

**Pay Range Disclosure**: No Mississippi state law requires salary ranges in job postings or on request.

**Equal Pay Law**: Miss. Code § 71-17-5 (Mississippi Equal Pay for Equal Work Act, effective July 1, 2022) prohibits employers with 5+ employees from paying employees of one sex less than the opposite sex for equal work requiring "equal skill, education, effort and responsibility" and performed under "similar working conditions." Applies to employers with 5+ employees. Exceptions: seniority systems, merit systems, production-based systems, or "any other factor other than sex" (including salary history and continuity of employment). **Note**: This is the only state equal-pay statute; race, ethnicity, and other protected-class pay discrimination are addressed only by federal law. Enforcement is private lawsuit only; no state agency investigates. Damages limited to back pay and attorney's fees (less than federal Equal Pay Act).

**Pay Data Reporting**: No Mississippi state law requires employers to report pay equity data. Federal EEO-1 reporting applies to employers with 100+ employees.

**Protected Classes**: Title VII, ADA, ADEA, Equal Pay Act, and other federal laws apply. No state-specific expansion of protected classes. Verify: No comprehensive state anti-discrimination statute exists for private employers; Title VII coverage gap for employers with < 15 employees.

## 5. Leave and Time: Paid Sick Leave, Family Leave, and Other Mandated Leave

**Paid Sick Leave**: No Mississippi state law mandates paid sick leave for private-sector employees. No accrual requirement. Employers may voluntarily provide sick leave; if they do, they must follow their own policies. State employees only receive 15 days/year sick leave.

**Paid Family and Medical Leave (PFMLA)**: No Mississippi state law mandates paid family leave or paid medical leave for private-sector employees. **Federal FMLA** (29 U.S.C. § 2601 et seq.) applies to covered employers (50+ employees within 75 miles), providing 12 weeks unpaid per 12-month period for qualifying reasons: serious health condition of employee or family member, childbirth, adoption, military exigency, or military caregiver leave. Employers must maintain health-insurance benefits during FMLA leave. Private-sector employees receive no state paid family leave.

**State Employees**: Mississippi public-sector employees (state employees only) receive 15 days/year sick leave, up to 12 weeks family leave, and beginning January 1, 2026, up to 8 weeks paid parental leave (state sector only). These protections do not extend to private-sector employees.

**Jury Duty Leave**: Miss. Code § 13-5-35 (Employment Protections for Jurors) protects employees from compulsory use of paid leave for jury service. Employers are **prohibited from requiring or requesting** an employee to use annual, vacation, or sick leave for time spent responding to a jury summons, participating in jury selection, or actually serving on jury duty. However, the statute does not require employers to provide compensated leave if the employer's policy does not otherwise provide such leave. An employer cannot retaliate against an employee for jury service. Violations constitute contempt of court. **Exception**: For employers with 5 or fewer full-time employees, courts must automatically postpone and reschedule jury service if another employee of that employer has already been summoned during the same period.

**Voting Leave**: Mississippi has **no voting leave law**. Employers are not required to provide paid or unpaid time off for employees to vote. However, employers are prohibited from interfering with employees' political rights. If an employee's regular working hours do not otherwise allow the employee time to vote, an employer may (but is not required to) allow the employee to take vacation or leave time to cast their vote.

**Military Leave and Re-employment**: Miss. Code § 33-1-19 (Re-employment Rights) protects members of reserve components of the Armed Forces or former service members discharged under non-dishonorable conditions. Employees who leave employment to perform duties or receive training with the Armed Forces of the United States or the State of Mississippi (including active state duty, state-training duty, or other military duty authorized under Title 10 or Title 32 of the United States Code) are entitled to be restored to their previous or a similar position with the same status, pay, and seniority. The period of absence is considered an absence with leave (may be without pay). If the employee remains qualified to perform job duties, reinstatement is required. No state paid military leave for private-sector employees.

**Holidays and PTO**: Entirely optional; no state mandate. Employers may set policies and communicate them at hire or on change. No state law requires holiday pay or PTO accrual.

**Interaction with Pay/Role Changes**: If an employee is promoted or base pay changes, existing leave balances (if any) are typically preserved. No state rule specifies mandatory carryover or payout on promotion. Employer policy and contract govern. On termination, Mississippi does not mandate payout of unused PTO/vacation/sick leave unless employer policy or contract specifically promises it. Federal FLSA governs only if accrued leave is considered earned wages under state law (verify applicability).

## 6. Records and Access: Retention, Employee Access, and Format Rules

**Payroll Record Retention Requirements**: Federal FLSA (29 U.S.C. § 211(b)) requires employers to retain payroll records for a minimum of 3 years. Mississippi has no state-specific mandate superseding the federal requirement. **Employers must retain:**

- Wage and hour records (hours worked, rate of pay, overtime)
- Deductions from wages (tax withholding, court orders, authorized deductions)
- Pay stubs and payroll documentation
- Records of final payment on termination
- E-Verify I-9 records (federal requirement, retain for 3 years from hire or 1 year from termination, whichever is later)
- Discipline and separation documentation
  Payroll records must be maintained in a form that allows for inspection and audit. Failure to retain required records exposes the employer to FLSA penalties.

**Personnel Files and Employee Access**: **Mississippi has NO statute granting employees the statutory right to access their own personnel files.** Private-sector employees have no legal right to review, inspect, or receive copies of their personnel records. Personnel files are not subject to Mississippi's Public Records Act (§ 25-1-100 et seq.); the public records exemption for personnel files is absolute for private employers. All access to personnel files is governed entirely by **employer policy alone**. Unlike some states (e.g., California, Illinois), Mississippi does not require employers to permit employee inspection of their files, even upon request. Public-sector employees may have different access rights under state personnel laws (verify for public employers).

**Maintenance and Format**: Mississippi has no state requirement mandating paper vs. electronic record storage. Employers may maintain records entirely in digital form if they comply with federal FLSA recordkeeping standards: records must be (1) accessible for inspection, (2) preserved in their original form or in a form that accurately reflects the original, and (3) retained for the applicable retention period. Employers using electronic systems (HRIS, payroll software, cloud-based platforms) must ensure backups are maintained and records are authentic, not subject to alteration or deletion during the retention period.

**Record Inspection and Audit**: Employees may not have a statutory right to inspect records, but the U.S. Department of Labor may audit payroll records during an FLSA investigation. Employers must cooperate with federal audit requests and produce records within the timeframe specified.

## 7. Privacy and Data: Employee Data, Biometric Monitoring, Breach Notification

**Comprehensive Data Privacy Law**: Mississippi has **no comprehensive state employee-data privacy statute** equivalent to CCPA or other state frameworks. There is no state agency dedicated to employment-data privacy enforcement, and no statutory employee consent or opt-out rights to data collection. Miss. Code § 75-24-29 (Data Breach Notification Law) is the only state data-protection rule affecting employers.

**Data Breach Notification (Miss. Code § 75-24-29)**: When an employer or a vendor acting on the employer's behalf that collects, maintains, or transmits "personal information" (defined as unencrypted SSN, driver's license number, state ID number, financial account number with security code, credit card number, or other similar unique identifier) experiences a breach of security that compromises the security or integrity of the personal information, the employer must notify affected individuals **in the most expedient time possible and without unreasonable delay**.

- **Notification Standard**: The statute uses the vague term "most expedient time possible and without unreasonable delay," offering no specific day count. Recommended best practice: within 3 business days of discovery to avoid disputes over reasonableness.
- **Notification Method**: Individual notice must be provided by mail, email, or phone, or by publication in a newspaper if the breach affects a large number of individuals.
- **Content**: Notification should describe the nature of the breach and the information compromised.
- **Law Enforcement Exception**: Notification may be delayed if law enforcement determines that notification would compromise a criminal investigation; notification must be provided once law enforcement agrees it will not interfere.
- **Credit Monitoring**: No state law requires employers to provide free credit monitoring; this is optional.
- **Enforcement and Damages**: Enforcement is by Mississippi Attorney General only; **no private right of action**. Employees cannot sue for breach-notification violations. Penalties are limited to Attorney General civil action and court-ordered restitution.

**Biometric Data and Facial Recognition**: Mississippi has **no state law regulating employee biometric collection** (fingerprints, facial recognition, iris scans, etc.) or use. Employers are free to collect biometric data for background checks, time-tracking, or security purposes without consent or disclosure, subject only to federal law (Title I of the Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA)) and common-law privacy tort (which provides limited protection). No state privacy policy, notice, or authorization requirement applies to biometric collection.

**Electronic Monitoring and Workplace Surveillance**: No Mississippi statute restricts electronic monitoring of employees (keystroke logging, email surveillance, location tracking, video surveillance in non-private areas, call recording). Federal ECPA (particularly the Wiretap Act and Stored Communications Act) and common-law privacy tort provide limited protection. Workplace surveillance, hidden cameras, and email monitoring are permitted under Mississippi law if employees are on notice or no reasonable expectation of privacy exists.

**Employee Data Residency and Cross-Border Processing**: Mississippi has **no data residency or cross-border processing restrictions**. Employers may store employee data on cloud servers located outside Mississippi or the United States without state-law approval. Federal FCRA (Fair Credit Reporting Act) and ordinary commercial data-protection practices (HIPAA for health data, GLB Act for financial data if applicable) apply.

**Social Media and Employee Monitoring**: Mississippi has **no statute protecting employees' personal social-media accounts or restricting employer access to or use of social-media information**. Employers may monitor employees' public social-media activity, request social-media passwords or account access, and use social-media information in hiring, promotion, or disciplinary decisions, subject only to federal discrimination laws (Title VII, ADA, ADEA) and common-law tort (which rarely succeeds).

## 8. Hiring and Background: Ban-the-Box, Background Checks, Drug Testing, E-Verify

**Ban-the-Box**: Mississippi has **no ban-the-box law**. Employers are permitted to inquire about criminal history on applications, interviews, or background checks at any time. Verify: No recent legislative changes as of 2026-09-03.

**Background Checks**: Permitted. Employers commonly use criminal-history checks via the Mississippi Department of Public Safety criminal history records or name-based inquiries. No state law restricts timing or scope for most private employers. Federal FCRA (Fair Credit Reporting Act) requires written consent and adverse-action disclosure.

**Drug Testing**: Pre-employment drug and alcohol testing is **permitted and optional** under Miss. Code § 71-7-3. The statute does not mandate testing; it permits employers to implement a testing program if they follow statutory procedural requirements.

- **Written Policy Requirement**: Employers must adopt and furnish a **written policy** to all affected employees and job applicants at least **30 days prior to implementation** of the testing program. The policy must contain specific components:
  - General statement of the employer's policy on employee drug and alcohol use
  - Identification of the grounds for testing and possible disciplinary action for a positive result
  - Statement advising employees of the statute's existence and protections
  - General statement regarding confidentiality of test results
  - Procedures for employees to confidentially report prescription or nonprescription medication use **before** testing
  - Circumstances under which testing may occur (e.g., pre-employment, reasonable suspicion, post-accident, random)
  - Which positions or categories of employees are subject to testing
  - Consequences of refusing to submit to testing
  - Information on assessment and rehabilitation opportunities (e.g., employee assistance program) following a confirmed positive test result
  - Statement that employees who receive a confirmed positive test may contest or explain the result
  - List of all drugs for which testing may be conducted
- **Applicant Notification**: Applicants must receive written notice of the testing policy and may be asked to sign an acknowledgment that they have received and understood the policy.
- **Test Requirement**: Refusal to submit to testing or a confirmed positive test result may be grounds for denial of employment or disciplinary action up to and including termination.
- **Test Types Permitted**: Miss. Code § 71-7-5 authorizes urinalysis, saliva tests, blood tests, and hair tests. Federal and state guidelines govern specific procedures and chain-of-custody requirements.
- **Confidentiality**: Test results must be kept confidential.
- **Government Employers**: For government employers, the decision to implement drug testing must be made by the executive head or governing body with authority to employ.

**E-Verify**: **Required for ALL employers, regardless of size.** Miss. Code § 71-11-3 (Mississippi Employment Protection Act) mandates verification of work eligibility via E-Verify (federal USCIS program) for all new employees. 1099 contractors are also covered. Employers who use E-Verify and follow the statute are **exempt from employment-authorization liability**, shielding them from civil suit or investigation based on hiring decisions made in good faith using E-Verify. Penalties for non-compliance: contract termination (for public employers/contractors), 3-year public-contract ineligibility, 1-year suspension of business licenses and permits. This is the most stringent state E-Verify requirement in the nation.

**Medical Cannabis and Employment**: Miss. Code §§ 41-137-1 to 41-137-67 (Mississippi Medical Cannabis Act, enacted 2022) provides employment protections for medical cannabis use by qualified patients. Under § 41-137-13:

- An employer **cannot refuse to hire, discharge, discipline, or take adverse employment action** against an individual based on the individual's **lawful medical use of cannabis** as authorized under the Medical Cannabis Act, regardless of whether the use impairs the employee.
- However, the law **does not require any employer to permit, accommodate, or allow** medical cannabis use in the workplace, or to modify job duties or working conditions.
- Critically, the law **does not restrict employers from maintaining drug-testing policies**, including testing for cannabis. An employer may test for cannabis and take adverse action based on a positive test result, even if the employee has a valid medical cannabis card.
- The protection applies only to off-duty, lawful medical use; workplace impairment may still be grounds for discipline.
- Enforcement is private lawsuit for prohibited employment actions; no state agency investigates.

**Firearms in Vehicles on Employer Property**: Miss. Code § 45-9-55 restricts employers' ability to regulate firearms in employee vehicles. A public or private employer **may not establish, maintain, or enforce any policy** that prohibits an employee from transporting or storing a **locked firearm in a locked vehicle** in a parking lot, parking garage, or designated parking area.

- **Exceptions**: A private employer **may prohibit** firearms in vehicles if the employer provides a parking facility with **restricted or limited access** (secured by gate, security station, or similar access-control measure that prevents general public access). Unrestricted public parking lots do not qualify; the employer must actively control who enters the lot.
- **Scope**: The rule applies only to locked firearms in locked vehicles; an employee may not carry or display a firearm in the workplace or in public areas of the workplace.
- **Employer Vehicles**: Excludes vehicles owned or leased by the employer and used by the employee in the course of business; employers may prohibit firearms in employer-owned vehicles.
- **Employer Immunity**: Employers are **protected from civil liability** for damages arising from the transportation, storage, possession, or use of firearms stored in vehicles under this section.
- **Federal and State Law Override**: The statute does not authorize firearm transportation or storage in locations where possession is prohibited by federal law or other state law (e.g., government buildings, schools, secure facilities).

## 9. Separation: WARN, Severance, Notice, and Noncompetes

**WARN Act**: The federal **Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act** (29 U.S.C. § 2102) requires covered employers (100+ employees) to provide **60 days' written notice** before plant closings or mass layoffs affecting 50+ workers in a 30-day period. Applies nationwide, including Mississippi. Failure to notify carries penalties.

**State Mini-WARN**: Mississippi has **no state mini-WARN law**. Federal WARN Act is the only mass-layoff notice requirement.

**Severance Pay**: No state law mandates severance. Severance is a matter of contract or employer discretion.

**Notice to Employee on Termination**: No state-specific notice period required; at-will employment permits immediate termination. Employers must follow any notice period specified in an employment contract or policy.

**Noncompete and Nonsolicitation Agreements**: Mississippi permits noncompete and nonsolicitation covenants under common law. **No state statute codifies noncompete enforceability**, so enforceability is determined entirely by Mississippi case law. The employer bears the burden of proving the agreement is: (1) reasonable in scope (restricted to legitimate business interests such as trade secrets, confidential information, customer lists, or goodwill), (2) reasonable in geographic area, and (3) reasonable in duration. Mississippi courts scrutinize these agreements closely; overly broad agreements may be unenforceable or subject to modification by a court. Courts have found that a 50-mile radius restriction was reasonable in certain contexts. Covenants not to solicit employees or customers are generally enforceable if reasonable in scope and duration. **Verify enforceability case-by-case**: Consult current Mississippi case law and the specific circumstances before enforcing, as enforceability is fact-dependent and evolving. An overly broad agreement may be void entirely (some jurisdictions apply the "blue-pencil" doctrine allowing courts to modify agreements; verify Mississippi's position).

**Workers' Compensation Retaliation**: Mississippi law protects employees from retaliation for filing a workers' compensation claim under the Mississippi Workers' Compensation Act. It is unlawful for an employer to discharge, demote, suspend, or otherwise retaliate against an employee for filing a workers' compensation claim or for exercising rights under workers' compensation law. An employee who is terminated or disciplined in retaliation may pursue a civil wrongful-termination or tort suit against the employer. Common-law tort claims (wrongful termination in violation of public policy) are available and enforceable. Employers should document legitimate, non-retaliatory reasons for any employment action taken against an injured employee.

## 10. Classification and Multi-State Rules

**Contractor vs. Employee Classification**: Mississippi applies the **Common Law Test** for unemployment-insurance and workers'-compensation purposes. The test centers on the right to control: does the business have the right to direct and control not just the final result but also how the work is done? Additional factors: the nature of the service (separate from employer's business), the duration (continuous vs. intermittent), and the degree of integration. **Default presumption**: A worker is presumed an employee under MS Code § 71-5-11(J); the party claiming independent-contractor status bears the burden.

**Remote Work and Multi-State Employees**: No Mississippi-specific rules govern remote workers or multi-state work. Federal FLSA applies to wages; state income-tax withholding is required if the employee works in Mississippi. Multi-state employers must comply with all applicable state laws in states where work is performed.

**Reciprocity**: Mississippi recognizes Mississippi law for employment disputes involving Mississippi-based work. Out-of-state employment contracts may invoke choice-of-law provisions, but if work is performed in Mississippi, Mississippi law applies to statutory protections.

### Promotion Workflow Summary

When HCM Next processes a promotion for a Mississippi-based employee:

1. Verify `eVerifyRequired: true` and E-Verify status is "verified."
2. If `employerHeadcount: 5+`, capture equal-pay audit trail: `payChangeReason`, job comparators, skill/effort/responsibility/working conditions, salary documentation.
3. If `employerType: manufacturer or public_service_corporation` AND `headcount: 50+`, validate new pay frequency is biweekly/semimonthly.
4. Preserve any accrued PTO/sick leave unless contract specifies forfeiture.
5. Check for noncompete; if present, flag as "enforceability uncertain; consult counsel."
6. If employee is on jury duty, exclude jury-duty time from PTO deductions.
7. No promotion-specific notice is required by state law.

### Base-Pay Change Workflow Summary

1. Document pay-change reason (merit, market, etc.) and business justification.
2. For sex-based pay comparisons, capture comparator job data to defend against § 71-17-5 disputes.
3. If `employerType: manufacturer or public_service_corporation` AND `headcount: 50+`, validate pay frequency remains biweekly/semimonthly.
4. If personal data is collected/modified, establish breach-notification protocol (≤3 days).
5. Retain all wage records and justification for 3 years.

### Termination Workflow Summary

1. At-will termination: no notice period required unless contract/policy specifies.
2. If `headcount: 100+` and separation count ≥50 in 30 days, file federal 60-day WARN notice.
3. Final paycheck: all earned wages, commissions, overtime by next regular payday (per employer pay schedule).
4. PTO payout: only if employer policy or contract requires; no state mandate.
5. If employee filed workers' compensation claim, verify termination reason is documented and unrelated to claim (retaliation risk).
6. If employee has noncompete, attach to record and flag enforceability as uncertain.
7. Retain all records for 3 years.

### Open Questions for Product Implementation

- Does the employer have E-Verify registration? If not, system must flag as non-compliant on hire.
- Does the employer have a written drug-testing policy (Miss. Code § 71-7-3)? If yes, store policy document and require 30-day notice before implementation.
- What is the employer's pay frequency? If manufacturer/public service 50+, must validate biweekly/semimonthly.
- Does the employer use noncompete agreements? If yes, attach and flag enforceability uncertainty.
- Is the employer covered by FMLA (50+ employees within 75 miles)? If yes, model 12-week unpaid leave entitlement for qualifying events.
- Does the employer track pay-equity data by sex? If yes, model audit trail for sex-based comparisons under § 71-17-5.
- What is the employer's breach-notification protocol? If personal data is handled, must notify affected parties within 3 days of discovery (best practice).
- Does the employer have restricted-access parking? If yes, can restrict firearms in vehicles (§ 45-9-55); if public parking, cannot.
