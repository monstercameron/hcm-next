# Montana Employment Law Research (2026-09-03)

## 1. Summary for HCM Next

- **NOT at-will after probation**: Montana is unique among U.S. states; after the default 12-month probationary period (or employer-specified period up to 18 months with extension), employers must have good cause to terminate. This fundamentally changes demotion, pay-cut, and termination workflows and represents a major departure from the at-will default. The Wrongful Discharge From Employment Act (WDEA, MCA §§ 39-2-901 to 39-2-915, enacted 1987) makes Montana the only state to abolish at-will employment by statute.
- **Probation period is default 12 months**: MCA § 39-2-904 (2021 amendment) sets 12 months as the default; employers may shorten it, eliminate it entirely, or extend once by up to 6 additional months (max 18 total if extended). During probation, at-will termination is permitted without good cause or justification.
- **Good cause definition not codified**: MCA § 39-2-904 requires good cause but does not define it in statute; courts interpret based on job performance deficiency, misconduct, attendance, business disruption, or other legitimate business reasons. Edge cases (cultural fit, marginal performance, general reductions) require legal review for defensibility.
- **Final pay timing**: MCA § 39-3-205 requires immediate payment on involuntary termination unless the employer has a written policy extending to the next payday or 15 days, whichever is sooner. Resignations allow next regular payday or 15 days from separation, whichever is sooner. Special rule: if alleged theft is involved, wages may be withheld if employee consents in writing or employer reports to law enforcement within 7 business days; if no charges within 30 days, wages must be paid in full.
- **Pay frequency: 10-day maximum hold**: MCA § 39-3-204 caps wage withholding at 10 business days after wages are earned and due; no frequency mandate (weekly, biweekly, semi-monthly all allowed). Employers must notify employees in writing of wage rate and pay date before hire.
- **Minimum wage ties to CPI-U annually**: MCA § 39-3-404; as of 2026-01-01, $10.85/hour (adjusted annually by inflation, rounded to nearest $0.05). Overtime is 1.5× for hours over 40 per week (MCA § 39-3-405).
- **Non-competes generally void**: MCA § 28-2-703 voids restraint-of-trade agreements except for sale of goodwill (§ 28-2-704) and partnership dissolution (§ 28-2-705); courts may enforce reasonable agreements in narrow cases (verify current case law; Snow Country Construction Inc. v. Laabs sets narrow exception for legitimately limited restraints).
- **Human Rights Act protected classes**: MCA § 49-2-303 prohibits discrimination on race, creed, religion, color, national origin, age, disability, marital status, or sex; 180-day complaint deadline to Montana Human Rights Bureau. Retaliation for complaints is prohibited.
- **Breach notification required**: MCA § 30-14-1704 mandates notice to affected residents "without unreasonable delay" when computerized personal information (SSN, financial account, driver's license, etc.) is breached. No specific day-count mandate; 3–5 days recommended.
- **No state paid sick leave, pay transparency, or salary-history ban**: Federal FLSA and FMLA apply; Montana has no mini-WARN law; employee access to personnel files is not statutorily mandated (common-law privacy principles apply). Proposed salary-history bans and pay-transparency bills have not passed as of 2026.

## 2. Employment Relationship: At-Will Status and Written Notices

Montana abolished at-will employment by statute for employees who complete a probationary period. MCA § 39-2-904 (Wrongful Discharge From Employment Act, MCA §§ 39-2-901 to 39-2-915) establishes that:

- **During probation**: Employment may be terminated at will by either party for any reason or no reason, on notice. At-will termination is permitted without good cause or advance justification during this window.
- **Default probationary period**: 12 months from hire (2021 amendment). Employers may set a shorter period, extend once by up to 6 months (total max 18 months), or eliminate probation entirely. Employers should document the probation length in writing and provide to the employee within 7 days of hire or at the time of hiring. The probation period should be clearly stated in any handbook, offer letter, or hiring document.
- **After probation**: Discharge is wrongful if not for good cause AND the employee has completed the employer's probationary period. This is the core of Montana's departure from at-will employment and represents a significant legal constraint on employer discretion.
- **Good cause**: Defined by case law (not the statute) as reasonable, job-related grounds for dismissal based on failure to satisfactorily perform job duties, disruption of the employer's operations, or other legitimate business reason. Managerial/supervisory employees have slightly broader employer discretion, though the good-cause requirement still applies. "Good cause" is fact-intensive and varies by role and circumstance; edge cases (e.g., poor cultural fit, minor performance gaps) require legal review.

**Written Notice at Hire**: Montana has no required written notice to the employee at hire about at-will status or wage-theft prevention; no state form mandated. However, best practice is to document the probation policy in writing and provide it to the employee. Employers should maintain written documentation of the probation policy in employee handbooks or offer letters.

**Internal Grievance Procedures and Exhaustion Requirement** (MCA § 39-2-912): If an employer maintains written internal procedures for appealing a discharge—other than those specified in § 39-2-912 (which has specific procedural safeguards)—the employee must exhaust those procedures before suing for wrongful discharge in court, subject to the 90-day completion rule. Failure to initiate or exhaust available internal procedures is an affirmative defense to a wrongful-discharge action; the burden is on the employee to show good cause for non-exhaustion. Critical timing: If the employer's internal procedures are not completed within 90 days from the date the employee initiates the appeal, the employee may file an action under Part 9 of Chapter 2 without further delay (the 90-day window is a safety valve). Employers must notify the employee of the grievance procedure within 7 days of discharge and supply the written policy to the employee. If the employer fails to notify the employee or does not follow its own written procedures (material breach), the exhaustion requirement is waived, and the employee may sue directly (§ 39-2-912). "Material violation" of a written personnel policy includes: (1) failure to provide written notice of discharge and appeal procedures within 7 days, (2) failure to allow a meaningful appeal opportunity, (3) failure to respond within the stated timeline, or (4) unilateral changes to the appeal procedure during the appeal process. Minor deviations (e.g., 8-day notice instead of 7, informal hearing instead of formal) do not trigger material-violation liability if the employee still has an opportunity to be heard.

## 3. Wages: Minimum, Overtime, Pay Frequency, Final Pay, and Deductions

**Minimum Wage** (MCA § 39-3-404): $10.85/hour as of 2026-01-01. Montana adjusts the minimum wage annually based on changes in the CPI-U (Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers) from August of the preceding year, rounded to the nearest $0.05. Employers must pay whichever is higher: Montana minimum or federal FLSA ($7.25/hour).

**Overtime** (MCA § 39-3-405): Employees must receive 1.5× regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. Farm workers are exempt. FLSA exemptions (executive, administrative, professional, outside sales) apply. No state-specific deviation from federal rules.

**Pay Frequency** (MCA § 39-3-204): Employers may not withhold earned wages for longer than 10 business days after wages are due and payable. No mandate on frequency (weekly, biweekly, semi-monthly, monthly all permitted). If an employer does not establish a pay period, the default is semi-monthly. Employers must notify employees in writing of the wage rate and payday before work begins.

**Final Paycheck on Termination** (MCA § 39-3-205):

- **Involuntary separation (layoff or discharge)**: All unpaid wages due immediately upon separation, unless the employer has a written personnel policy extending payment to the next regular payday or 15 days from separation, whichever is sooner. "Unpaid wages" include: (1) regular wages for hours worked through the date of separation, (2) accrued overtime at 1.5× for all hours over 40 in the final week, (3) accrued vacation/PTO per employer policy (if the policy requires payout), and (4) accrued sick leave if the employer's policy provides for payout. If alleged theft is involved, wages may be withheld if the employee consents in writing or the employer reports the alleged theft to law enforcement within 7 business days of the discharge; if no charges are filed within 30 days, wages must be paid in full. Wage withholding for alleged theft must be narrowly tailored and the basis for withholding must be documented.
- **Resignation**: Unpaid wages due on the next regular payday for the pay period in which the employee worked through the separation date or 15 days from separation, whichever occurs first. Resignation final pay is generally treated more liberally; the 15-day outside date applies even if it exceeds the next regular payday by a few days.

**Deductions and Pay Statements**: Montana follows federal FLSA rules; only deductions required by law, court order, or written employee authorization are permitted. Wage deductions for breakage, damage, or shortage are prohibited unless the employer proves negligence or willful misconduct. No state-specific pay-statement content requirement beyond federal FLSA (employee name, pay period, hours worked, rate, gross/net pay, deductions; federal FLSA record-keeping applies). Employers must provide itemized pay stubs if required by policy or contract. Deductions for required uniforms, tools, or training fees are restricted; the employer must ensure the deduction does not reduce the employee's pay below minimum wage. Wage orders and court-ordered garnishments must be honored; failure to remit garnished wages may expose the employer to liability. Deductions for hypothetical losses or future damages (e.g., "if you break something in the future") are not permitted; only documented actual losses qualify for withholding.

## 4. Pay Transparency and Equity

**Salary History Bans**: Montana has NO salary-history ban. Employers may inquire into and rely upon applicants' prior wages. No state law restricts this. Bills proposed in 2023 (SB 146, Wage Opportunity and Transparency Act) and 2026 (SB 313) would have restricted inquiries, but neither passed.

**Pay Range Disclosure**: Montana has NO pay transparency law. Employers are not required to disclose salary ranges in job postings or on request.

**Equal Pay Law**: MCA § 39-3-104 (Equal Pay for Women for Equivalent Service) prohibits employers from paying women less than men for equivalent service or the same class of work in the same industry, school, establishment, office, or place of employment. The statute applies to work requiring "the same or essentially the same knowledge, skills, efforts and responsibilities" (verify precise definition and case law on what constitutes equivalent service). Violations are misdemeanors (fine $25–$500). This is narrower in scope than the federal Equal Pay Act (which is gender-neutral) and is sex-specific to women; **verify** whether case law has expanded this or whether gender-neutral pay equity is covered under MCA § 49-2-303 (Human Rights Act) instead. Employers may defend equal-pay claims by demonstrating a seniority system, merit system, production system, or factor other than sex as the basis for the pay difference.

**Pay Data Reporting**: Montana has no state pay-equity data reporting requirement. Federal EEO-1 reporting applies to employers with 100+ employees; pay-grade data is tracked at the federal level only.

**Protected Classes** (MCA § 49-2-303, Montana Human Rights Act): Title VII (race, color, religion, sex, national origin), ADA (disability), ADEA (age), and Montana-specific protections (marital status, creed) all apply. Montana Human Rights Bureau enforces complaints; 180-day complaint deadline from the date of discrimination. Bona fide occupational qualifications (BFOQ) must be narrowly construed and directly related to the essential functions of the job. Employers must avoid pay decisions based on protected characteristics and maintain documentation of job-related pay decisions (e.g., performance reviews, salary surveys, role level assessments). Retaliation against employees who complain of discrimination is prohibited.

## 5. Leave and Time: Paid Sick Leave, Family Leave, and Other Mandated Leave

**Montana State Mandate**: NO state law requires paid sick leave, paid family leave, or paid time off for private-sector employees. This is entirely optional and left to employer discretion.

**Federal FMLA**: 12 weeks unpaid per 12-month period for qualifying reasons (serious health condition of employee or family member, childbirth/adoption, military exigency, military caregiver leave) applies to covered employers (50+ employees within 75 miles, 12+ months employed, 1,250+ hours in past 12 months). Employers must maintain health-insurance benefits during FMLA leave and restore the employee to the same or an equivalent position upon return. FMLA leave is job-protected; failure to restore or maintain benefits violates federal law. Employers should coordinate FMLA leave with any company-provided paid leave policies (e.g., vacation, sick leave may run concurrently with FMLA if company policy provides).

**Montana Public Employees**: State employees receive 15 days/year sick leave and up to 12 weeks family leave, but these are state-employee policies, not private-sector mandates. Private employers are not required to offer these benefits.

**Holidays and PTO**: Entirely optional; no state mandate. Employers may set policies and communicate them at hire or change. If an employer offers voluntary PTO or sick leave, the policy should clearly state whether leave is forfeited upon termination or paid out. Employers may choose to offer unlimited PTO, capped PTO, or no PTO at all; however, once a policy is adopted, it must be applied consistently to avoid claims of discrimination or contract breach. If an employee is transferred to a different role or pay level, existing PTO balances should be preserved unless the employment contract or policy specifies otherwise (e.g., a policy stating "PTO is forfeited upon demotion" would need to be explicit and consistently applied).

**Interaction with Pay/Role Changes**: If an employee is promoted or has a base-pay change, existing accrued leave (if any) should be preserved unless the employment contract or policy explicitly specifies forfeiture. No state rule specifies carryover, use-it-or-lose-it, or payout rules; **verify** employer policy and industry practice. On termination or resignation, Montana does not require payout of unused accrued vacation/PTO unless the employer's written policy or employment contract provides for it (this is an employer-election rule, not a mandate; however, once a policy is adopted, it must be consistently applied to avoid claims of discrimination or breach of contract).

## 6. Records and Access: Retention, Employee Access, and Format Rules

**Payroll Record Retention**: Employers must retain payroll records and documents related to remuneration for at least 4 years after the calendar year in which remuneration was paid (federal FLSA requires 3 years; Montana's 4-year rule is more stringent and applies). Montana Admin. Rule 2.21.3104 addresses final pay record retention.

**Personnel Files and Employee Access**: Montana has no codified personnel-file statute requiring employers to maintain personnel files, provide access, or follow specific procedures for file review or employee disputes. General principles suggest employees may request access to their own personnel records on reasonable notice (e.g., 5–10 business days), but this is not mandatory by statute. Employers should adopt a clear policy for employee access to personnel records, including what may be withheld (e.g., references from third parties, confidential internal evaluations, other employees' names in complaints) and the timeline for providing copies (usually 10–15 days). Some records (e.g., medical, safety investigation details) may be segregated and access may be limited. **verify** current case law on employee privacy rights in Montana, as common-law privacy tort principles may apply.

**Record Format**: No state requirement for paper vs. electronic. Employers may maintain records digitally provided they remain accessible, authentic, and not subject to loss (federal FLSA rules apply). Cloud storage is permitted but the employer remains responsible for retention, security, and breach notification per MCA § 30-14-1704.

**Separation Records**: Employers should retain termination notices (discharge letter, reason for termination), final pay records (calculation, check or direct-deposit record), and internal grievance documentation (appeal letter, response, outcome) as part of the 4-year payroll retention cycle. These records protect both parties in case of wrongful-discharge litigation and serve as evidence of good-cause justification.

## 7. Privacy and Data: Employee Data, Biometric Monitoring, Breach Notification

**Data Privacy Law**: Montana has no comprehensive state data privacy statute equivalent to CCPA or GDPR. However, MCA § 30-14-1704 (Computer Security Breach) imposes breach-notification requirements.

**Breach Notification** (MCA § 30-14-1704): When an employer (or processor) that owns or licenses computerized data including personal information (SSN, driver's license, financial account, payment card, bank account number) experiences a breach, the employer must notify affected Montana residents "without unreasonable delay" and consistent with law enforcement needs. No specific day-count mandate in statute; "without unreasonable delay" is the legal standard (good practice: notify within 3–5 business days to avoid disputes over reasonableness; verify current guidance from Montana Department of Justice). Notification may be delayed if law enforcement determines it will impede a criminal investigation, but notification must be provided once the agency determines notification will not compromise the investigation. Breach notification must include details of the breach, the types of information disclosed, steps the employer is taking to secure systems, and resources available to affected parties.

**Montana Consumer Data Privacy Act (MCDPA)** (effective Oct. 1, 2024): Applies to for-profit businesses collecting personal information of Montana residents. Employee records are generally exempt: personal information relating to employees, applicants, independent contractors, and beneficiaries whose communications/transactions occur within the employment role (including emergency contacts and benefits) is excluded from MCDPA coverage. This means employee data collected for payroll, HR, and benefits administration is not subject to MCDPA consumer rights (access, deletion, opt-out). Verify if this exemption applies if employee data is used for non-employment purposes (e.g., marketing, analytics outside the employment context).

**Biometric Data and Monitoring**: No Montana-specific statute regulates employee biometric collection (e.g., fingerprints for background checks, facial recognition, iris scans) or electronic monitoring (e.g., keystroke logging, GPS tracking, email monitoring). Federal ECPA (Wiretap Act, Stored Communications Act) restricts interception of electronic communications; Montana common-law privacy torts (invasion of privacy) apply to egregious monitoring. Employers should maintain a clear policy on monitoring and data collection, obtain written consent where possible, and ensure monitoring is job-related and proportionate. Monitoring in high-privacy areas (bathrooms, locker rooms) is prohibited under common law.

**Employee Data Residency/Processing**: No state-specific restrictions on where employee data is stored or processed; federal law (FCRA for background checks, ECPA for communications) and ordinary commercial data-protection practices apply. Employers using cloud providers for payroll or HR data should ensure the provider has adequate security and breach-notification provisions in its contract.

## 8. Hiring and Background: Ban-the-Box, Background Checks, Drug Testing, E-Verify

**Ban-the-Box**: Montana has NO ban-the-box law. Employers may inquire about criminal history on applications, during interviews, or via background check at any time in the hiring process.

**Background Checks**: Permitted. Employers may use criminal-history checks via Montana Crime Information Center (GCIC) or third-party providers with fingerprints or name-based consent. No state law restricts timing or scope for most private employers.

**Drug Testing** (MCA § 39-2-205 to 39-2-211, Workforce Drug and Alcohol Testing Act): Permitted, but only for employees in qualifying positions:

- Hazardous work environments (e.g., mining, construction with fall risk, chemical handling)
- Security positions (armed or unarmed security personnel)
- Positions affecting public safety or public health (e.g., healthcare workers, first responders, child-care workers)
- Positions requiring driving a motor vehicle (commercial drivers, delivery drivers, etc.)
- Positions involving fiduciary responsibility (e.g., financial officers, trustees)

Employers must adopt a written policy and procedures, make the policy available to all subject employees, and provide 60 days' advance notice before testing is implemented (MCA § 39-2-207). Testing must meet chain-of-custody and procedural standards at least as stringent as 49 CFR Part 40 (DOT standards). A medical review officer (trained in substance abuse and toxicology) must review and certify positive results—and consult with the employee about any legitimate explanation (e.g., prescription medication)—before any employer action (discipline, termination, denial of hire). Pre-employment testing is permitted only if the position qualifies under the above categories. Random testing during employment must be specified in the written policy. Post-accident testing (after a workplace injury) is limited and must be reasonable in timing and scope. Reasonable suspicion testing requires documented observation by a trained supervisor. Montana does not require testing for marijuana use post-hire in compliance with federal law, but employers may test if the policy is clear and consistently applied. **verify**: Current Montana case law and MCDPA implications for drug-testing data collection.

**E-Verify**: No Montana state mandate equivalent to some other states' requirements for private employers. Federal E-Verify requirements apply to federal contractors and certain industries (government, certain healthcare). Private employers not subject to federal E-Verify should still verify work eligibility via Form I-9 (USCIS) and consider E-Verify as a best practice. Consult federal requirements (IRCA, I-9, E-Verify portals) if the employer has federal contracts or operates in regulated industries.

## 9. Separation: WARN, Severance, Notice, and Noncompetes

**WARN Act**: The federal Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act (29 U.S.C. § 2102) applies nationwide, including in Montana. Employers with 100+ full-time employees (on the payroll for the 12 months preceding notice) must provide 60 days' written notice before plant closures or mass layoffs. "Mass layoff" is defined as a reduction in force affecting 50+ employees in a 30-day period at a single site, or 50-499 employees if this constitutes at least one-third of the workforce at that site. Notification must be given to: (1) each affected employee not represented by a union, (2) each union representative if applicable, and (3) the chief elected official of the unit of local government where the layoff will occur. Failure to provide 60-day notice triggers liability for back pay and benefits for up to 60 days per affected employee. Montana has NO state-level "mini-WARN" law requiring additional notice for smaller layoffs below the federal threshold, so employers with fewer than 50–100 employees are not subject to mass-layoff notice requirements unless they are federal contractors or operate in specific regulated industries.

**Severance Pay**: No state law mandates severance. Severance is a matter of contract or employer discretion.

**Notice to Employee on Termination**: No state-specific notice period is required beyond at-will principles (during probation) or good-cause documentation (after probation). However, employers must notify the employee of any internal grievance procedure within 7 days of discharge and provide the written policy. If the employer does not follow its own written internal procedures (e.g., promised appeal steps, hearing, notice), the employee may sue under § 39-2-912 (Material violation of express personnel policy). Material violations include breach of promised notice, failure to follow documented procedures for appeals/reviews, or failure to provide the employee opportunity to be heard. Violations of minor policies (e.g., not providing a written reason statement when the statute does not require it) do not trigger wrongful-discharge liability.

**Noncompete and Nonsolicitation Agreements** (MCA § 28-2-703): Montana law provides that "any contract by which anyone is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade, or business of any kind is to that extent void," with limited exceptions:

- Sale of goodwill of a business (MCA § 28-2-704): Noncompete restrictions are enforceable if the buyer is restrained from engaging in the same business within the acquired territory for a reasonable time.
- Dissolution of partnership (MCA § 28-2-705): Restraints on ex-partners are enforceable if reasonable.

Noncompete agreements for employment are generally unenforceable. However, Montana courts (notably in Snow Country Construction Inc. v. Laabs, 1999 MT 193) have recognized narrow exceptions for reasonable restraints (limited duration, geographic scope, legitimate business interest such as protection of trade secrets or customer relationships). Courts scrutinize these closely and will not enforce overly broad agreements. **verify**: Consult current Montana Supreme Court case law and recent decisions before relying on noncompete enforceability; enforce only when narrow exceptions (goodwill sale or partnership) clearly apply. Nonsolicitation agreements (non-poaching of customers or employees) are similarly subject to scrutiny for reasonableness; some courts have treated these differently from noncompetes, but uncertainty remains. Do not enforce non-competes without legal review; risk of wrongful termination claim if employee opposes enforcement and alleges retaliation.

## 10. Classification and Multi-State Rules

**Contractor vs. Employee Classification**: Montana Employment Security Law (MCA § 34-9-2) defines employee/employment for unemployment-insurance and wage-and-hour purposes. Classification is based on the **true underlying nature of the working relationship**, not labels or the parties' characterization. Relevant factors include: control over work methods and performance (greater control = employee); provision of tools and materials (employer provision = employee); integration into business operations (integrated work = employee); permanence and duration of relationship (indefinite or long-term = employee); investment in business by worker (independent contractor typically invests more); opportunity for profit/loss (independent contractor bears more risk); provision of benefits (employer provision = employee). Misclassification of employees as independent contractors triggers wage-reporting errors, payroll tax liabilities, and may expose employers to audit by Montana Department of Labor & Industry and federal agencies (IRS, Department of Labor). Wrongful misclassification may also trigger wrongful-discharge claims if the "contractor" is terminated without good cause and claims employee status. Courts apply a strict test; burden is on the employer to prove independent-contractor status.

**Remote Work and Multi-State Employees**: No Montana-specific rules govern remote workers or employees working partly in Montana. Federal FLSA applies to wages, hours, and overtime. Montana income-tax withholding is required if the employee works in Montana or is a Montana resident (verify with Montana Department of Revenue). Multi-state employers must comply with all applicable state laws where work is performed; this includes the wrongful-discharge act (if the employee works in Montana after probation), pay frequency and final-pay rules (MCA § 39-3-204 to 39-3-205), and data-privacy rules (MCA § 30-14-1704). Coordinate state requirements when an employee relocates or starts remote work.

**Reciprocity and Choice of Law**: Montana recognizes Montana law for employment disputes when work is performed in Montana. Out-of-state employment contracts may invoke choice-of-law provisions (e.g., "this employment is governed by the law of State X"), but if work is performed in Montana, Montana law (including the wrongful-discharge statute, MCA §§ 39-2-901 to 39-2-915, and pay rules, MCA § 39-3-204 to 39-3-205) applies to the extent it provides stronger protections than the chosen law. Courts may not enforce a choice-of-law provision that deprives a Montana-based employee of Montana statutory protections (e.g., good-cause requirement after probation).

## 11. Implications for HCM Next (P1A/P1B)

When the platform processes a promotion, base-pay change, manager change, or termination for a worker based in Montana, the following obligations apply:

**Probation Tracking and Expiration**:

- Record the probation end date in the employee profile (default 12 months from hire, or employer-specified up to 18 months if extension is documented). Store this as a computed field or explicit timestamp.
- After probation expires, flag or tag the employee record as "Post-Probation" and apply good-cause requirement rule. This changes the risk profile of pay cuts, demotions, or role reductions and must gate certain workflows.
- Provide a calendar alert or compliance reminder to the employer as probation nears expiration (e.g., 30 days before, 1 day before) to ensure intentional decisions are made if probation is to be extended or if the employee is to be retained post-probation.
- When initiating a termination after probation has expired, the platform must **require** a good-cause justification field (e.g., dropdown: "Performance deficiency", "Misconduct", "Attendance/punctuality", "Operational restructuring", "Other—specify"). Do not allow termination workflow to complete without good-cause entry. Document the good-cause justification in the termination record for audit and litigation readiness.
- If probation has NOT expired, allow at-will termination (no good-cause required), but prompt the employer to confirm: "This employee is in probation period (expires [date]). Termination allowed without cause, but confirm: Proceed?" to ensure intentionality.

**Pay Changes During and After Probation**:

- **During probation**: Pay changes (raises, cuts) do not trigger the good-cause requirement per se. At-will termination is permitted if the employee rejects a pay cut. However, extreme cuts, demotions, or reductions paired with other factors (protected-class status, union activity, complaints) may expose the employer to discrimination or retaliation claims. Document the business reason for pay changes (market adjustment, performance, role change, cost control) for audit.
- **After probation**: A significant pay cut or demotion (e.g., >15% reduction, reduction in title/responsibility) must be justifiable as good cause to terminate or as a legitimate role change. If the employee rejects the new terms (e.g., refuses demotion or pay cut), treat as a separation event: (1) If the employer insists on the change and the employee refuses, document the refusal; (2) If the employer then terminates for refusal, good cause is: "Refusal to accept job-level/pay change"; however, **verify** whether this is defensible as "good cause" under case law—it may be deemed constructive termination if the change was unilateral and forced. (3) If the pay cut is retaliatory (e.g., in response to a complaint or protected activity), it is wrongful discharge and unlawful regardless of probation status. Flag for legal review if the change is unilateral, substantial, and follows a complaint or protected activity. Recommendation: coordinate significant pay cuts or demotions with HR/legal before implementing; offer a grace period (30–60 days) for the employee to accept or propose alternatives before termination.

**Final Pay Calculation and Timing**:

- On involuntary termination (layoff, discharge): Calculate final pay (all earned wages, overtime at 1.5×, plus accrued vacation/PTO per employer policy).
- Check for employer's written final-pay policy; if it extends payment to next payday or 15 days (whichever sooner), apply that grace period. If no policy, payment must be immediate.
- On resignation: Final pay due next regular payday or within 15 days of separation, whichever is sooner.
- Flag wage-withholding violations: no hold longer than 10 business days after wages are earned and due.

**Leave Balance Preservation**:

- On promotion: Preserve existing accrued PTO/sick leave unless employment contract or written policy provides for forfeiture. Document any policy change.
- On pay change: No requirement to modify leave balances unless the role change alters the accrual rate or eligibility (e.g., movement from hourly to salaried); document the change.
- On termination: If employer policy requires payout of unused accrued vacation, calculate and include in final pay (verify employer policy; Montana does not mandate this).

**Internal Grievance Procedure and Legal Liability** (MCA § 39-2-912):

- If the employer has written internal procedures for appealing a discharge (other than those in § 39-2-912), the employee must exhaust them before suing for wrongful discharge. This is a mandatory exhaustion requirement.
- Completion deadline: 90 days from the date the employee initiates the appeal. If procedures are not completed within 90 days, the employee may file an action in court without waiting further.
- Notification requirement: The employer must notify the employee of the grievance procedure **within 7 days of discharge** and provide the written policy. Failure to notify waives exhaustion; the employee may sue directly.
- Platform model: Implement a discharge-notification workflow that triggers a 7-day deadline to send the employee the grievance policy and initiate a formal appeal process. Track: (1) date of discharge, (2) date policy was provided, (3) date employee initiated appeal (if any), (4) appeal start date, (5) appeal completion date, (6) outcome. Ensure platform workflow enforces the 90-day completion window and alerts if procedures are stalling.
- If the employer fails to follow its own written personnel policy (e.g., promised 48-hour notice before termination, appeal opportunity, written response within 14 days), the employee may sue for material violation of the expressed policy (§ 39-2-912) without exhausting procedures or reaching 90 days. "Material violation" means a substantial breach that deprived the employee of a fair opportunity to appeal or defend themselves; minor delays or informal variations do not trigger liability.
- Legal compliance: Model these procedures in the platform and ensure they are documented and followed; non-compliance exposes the employer to wrongful-discharge liability and bypasses the statute's exclusive remedy framework.

**Probation Extension and Demotion**:

- If an employer extends probation (up to 6 months additional, max 18 months total per MCA § 39-2-904), ensure the extension is documented **in writing** and communicated to the employee **before the initial probation period expires**. Late notice (after the period has expired) does not extend probation; the at-will period ends on the original date unless extended before expiration. Platform model: Alert employer 30 days before probation expiration; require explicit "extend probation" decision with new end date and written notice to employee.
- During an extended probation, at-will termination is still permitted; no good cause is required.
- If an employer demotes an employee after probation (e.g., role reduction, pay cut) without the employee's voluntary agreement, this may be treated as a termination event under case law. If the employee does not accept the new terms and the employer insists, the employee may claim "constructive termination" (forced resignation) if the change is material (e.g., significant pay cut, loss of supervisory role). To enforce a demotion post-probation: (1) Document the business reason (reorganization, performance, cost control); (2) Offer the new role and give the employee 14–30 days to accept or decline; (3) If the employee declines, treat as resignation by the employee or termination by the employer (decide which is factually accurate); (4) If the employer terminates for the decline, document good cause: "Refusal to accept reassigned role" or "Inability to perform at new level" (verify if this meets the good-cause threshold; edge case). Recommendation: Secure legal review before implementing involuntary demotions post-probation.

**Other Constraints**:

- **Discrimination and Protected Classes** (MCA § 49-2-303): Ensure pay changes and terminations do not violate discrimination statutes. Document comparative data (comparable roles, responsibilities, pay levels) for audit trails and defense in equal-pay disputes. Apply pay changes consistently across similar roles; if pay cuts fall disproportionately on protected classes, scrutiny will apply. Retaliation for complaints of discrimination is prohibited; any termination following a discrimination complaint is presumed retaliatory unless clearly justified.
- **Non-compete enforcement** (MCA § 28-2-703): Flag any non-compete agreements in the employee file. Montana generally voids noncompete agreements for employment; only enforce in narrow goodwill-sale or partnership-dissolution contexts. Do not enforce an employment noncompete without legal review and current case law confirmation. If an employee opposes noncompete enforcement and the employer terminates for the opposition, risk of wrongful-discharge and retaliation claims is high.
- **Employee References and Written Reason Statements** (MCA § 39-2-801): If an employee demands a written statement of reasons for discharge in writing (in person, email, or letter), the employer must provide it within a reasonable time (typically 5–10 business days). Failure to provide a written statement when the employee has requested it may expose the employer to blacklisting liability: the employer becomes liable if it later provides a statement of the reason to a third party (e.g., a prospective employer) without having first provided a written statement to the employee on demand. Best practice: always provide a written reason statement at discharge; proactive notice avoids the trap.
- **Data Breach and Personal Information** (MCA § 30-14-1704): If employee personal data (SSN, financial account, driver's license, etc.) is breached or exposed in a security incident, trigger notification to affected employees "without unreasonable delay." Recommended timeline: within 3–5 business days of discovery or reasonable belief that a breach occurred. Delay increases risk of "unreasonable" characterization. If law enforcement requests a delay to preserve a criminal investigation, honor the request but notify once the agency clears notification.

**Open Questions and Configuration Checklist for Employer**:

The platform should prompt employers to answer the following questions when setting up Montana rules, and store the answers as configuration flags to govern workflows:

1. **Probation Policy**: Does the employer have a written probation policy? What is the specified probation period (default 12 months if not stated; options: shorter, 12 months, or extended up to 18 months)? Is the probation period stated in the employee handbook, offer letter, or employment agreement? _Platform action_: Require employer to specify and document probation period; default to 12 months if not set.

2. **Internal Grievance Procedures**: Does the employer have written internal procedures for appealing a discharge (formal HR review, management appeal, arbitration, etc.)? Are these procedures documented in a handbook or separate policy? _Platform action_: If yes, store the procedure description, appeal deadline, and notification requirements; enforce the 7-day notice rule and 90-day completion deadline.

3. **Final Pay Policy**: Does the employer have a written final-pay policy? Does it require immediate payment on discharge, or does it extend payment to the next regular payday or 15 days? _Platform action_: Store the policy choice and apply it in final-pay calculation workflows; default to immediate if no policy.

4. **Accrued Leave Payout**: Does the employer have a policy on payout or forfeiture of accrued vacation/PTO on termination, resignation, or change of role? _Platform action_: Store the policy and apply it to separation calculations and role-change workflows.

5. **Non-Compete Agreements**: Does the employer use non-compete or non-solicit agreements? If so, document the context (are they tied to sale of business, partnership, or employment?). _Platform action_: Flag agreements as generally unenforceable; require legal review before enforcing; do not enforce without explicit legal approval.

6. **Federal Coverage - WARN Act**: Is the employer subject to federal WARN (100+ full-time employees)? If so, what is the employer's process for providing 60-day notice of mass layoffs (50+ employees)? _Platform action_: If covered, enforce 60-day notice requirement in mass-layoff workflows and flag for coordination with state rules.

7. **Federal Coverage - FMLA**: Is the employer subject to federal FMLA (50+ employees within 75 miles)? If so, how does the employer handle leave eligibility, job restoration, and benefits continuation? _Platform action_: If covered, coordinate FMLA leave with Montana rules (e.g., if an employee is on FMLA leave and a pay change occurs, ensure job restoration and benefits continuity).

8. **Drug Testing Compliance**: Does the employer conduct drug or alcohol testing? If so, for which positions (hazardous, security, driving, safety-sensitive, fiduciary)? Does the employer have a written policy and 60-day notice? _Platform action_: If testing is conducted, require documented policy, confirm 60-day advance notice was given, track positive results and medical review officer review; do not allow termination based on positive test without MRO certification and employee consultation.

9. **Equal Pay Monitoring**: Does the employer monitor pay equity by role, level, and demographic group? Does the employer have a policy for regular pay equity audits? _Platform action_: Recommend pay-equity analysis tools; log pay changes by role and gender/protected class for audit trails; flag pay reductions disproportionately affecting protected classes.

10. **Breach Notification Procedures**: If employee data is breached, does the employer have a process for notifying affected individuals? What is the target timeline (e.g., 3–5 business days)? _Platform action_: Implement breach-notification workflow; set default deadline of 3 business days from discovery; escalate if timeline is at risk.

### Summary of Unique Montana Constraints

To summarize, Montana's employment law regime creates three critical compliance obligations for HCM Next:

1. **Probation-to-good-cause transition**: The platform must track probation end dates and enforce a mandatory good-cause requirement for all terminations after probation expires. This is the defining feature of Montana employment law and requires explicit workflow gates.

2. **Internal grievance exhaustion**: If the employer has documented appeal procedures, the platform must enforce a 90-day exhaustion deadline and track compliance with the 7-day notification requirement. Non-compliance waives exhaustion and exposes the employer to wrongful-discharge liability without the safe harbor of these procedures.

3. **Final pay timing**: The platform must calculate and enforce final-pay requirements (immediate for discharge unless written policy extends to next payday or 15 days for voluntary separation). Violations carry penalties and create wage-theft claims.

These three elements—probation tracking, grievance procedure enforcement, and final-pay calculation—are the core HCM Next obligations for Montana compliance. All other rules (discrimination, non-competes, drug testing, data breach notification) are standard employment-law constraints that apply in most states; Montana adds the probation-good-cause framework as a structural difference from at-will employment.

## Notes on Verification

The following topics could not be fully verified with available sources and require legal review or clarification:

1. **Good-cause definition edge cases**: Montana statute does not define "good cause" for discharge after probation; case law interpretation is fact-intensive. Verify what constitutes good cause in specific situations (e.g., marginal performance, cultural fit, personality conflicts, general business reductions). Courts have upheld terminations for "legitimate business reason" broadly, but boundaries remain unclear.

2. **Montana Equal Pay Act scope**: MCA § 39-3-104 is sex-specific (prohibiting pay discrimination against women). Unclear whether gender-neutral pay equity is covered separately under MCA § 49-2-303 (Human Rights Act). Verify whether the state's human rights law extends to gender-neutral equal pay.

3. **Non-compete case law**: Montana courts have recognized narrow exceptions to the noncompete voidance statute (Snow Country Construction, 1999), but recent case law (2020–2026) on enforceability and specific fact patterns should be reviewed before enforcing any agreement.

4. **Personnel file access rights**: Montana has no statutory personnel-file statute. Employee access rights are inferred from common law and the Open Records Act principles, but specifics (timeline for providing records, what may be withheld, employee inspection vs. copy rights) are not codified. Verify employer's local practice and any employee handbook provisions.

5. **Breach notification timeline**: MCA § 30-14-1704 requires notification "without unreasonable delay" but does not specify a day count. 3–5 days is a recommended timeline based on "expedient" language in the statute, but verify with Montana Department of Justice or recent case law if disputes arise.

6. **Drug-testing post-hire limitations**: MCA §§ 39-2-205 et seq. permit testing only for employees in qualifying positions. Random testing, reasonable-suspicion testing, and post-accident testing are permitted but have procedural requirements. Verify specific testing protocols and limitations for your employer's positions.

7. **Paid vacation payout on termination**: Montana does not mandate payout of accrued vacation on termination or resignation; payout is optional unless the employer's written policy provides for it. Verify the employer's written policy on whether accrued vacation is forfeited or paid out; once established, the policy must be consistently applied.
