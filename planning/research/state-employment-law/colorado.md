# Colorado Employment Law

**State:** Colorado | **Researched:** 2026-09-03 | **Status:** REVIEWED

## 1. Summary for HCM Next

- **Job postings and promotions** must include hourly or salary range, benefits description, and application-window close date; employers must notify Colorado employees of job opportunities before selection and then notify colleagues of who was hired/promoted within 30 calendar days (EPEWA, CRS § 8-5-101 et seq., amended Jan 1, 2024). Application Fairness Act (CRS § 8-2-131, effective Aug 7, 2023) prohibits requesting or considering applicant age or graduation date.
- **Base-pay changes** cannot use applicant's salary history; employers cannot enforce pay-secrecy policies; equal-pay rules (CRS § 8-5-101 et seq.) prohibit paying employees of one sex less than another sex for substantially similar work. Pay ranges must be disclosed on or shortly after posting.
- **Manager changes** that constitute promotions or job opportunities trigger the notification and posting requirements above; a manager transfer of the same employee may not; verify scope.
- **Termination** requires immediate final-pay delivery if involuntary (or within 6 hours of next business day if payroll office closed), or payment on next regular payday if resignation; accrued vacation must be paid out within 14 days of written demand (no "use-it-or-lose-it" policies allowed); separate unemployment-insurance notice must be provided with reason for termination (SB 22-234, CRS § 8-90-107).
- **Wage theft penalties** (SB 22-161, HB 25-1001): willful underpayment can trigger treble damages, civil penalties, and enforcement action by Colorado Department of Labor and Employment. Wage claims subject to strict compliance.
- **Paid leave** obligations: 48 hours paid sick leave per year (Healthy Families and Workplaces Act, CRS § 8-13.3-401 et seq.), plus 12 weeks paid family/medical leave if eligible under Colorado FAMLI (family members, newborn, or personal medical condition; employer or private plan required); premiums 0.88% in 2026 with $184,500 annual wage cap.
- **Payroll records** and personnel files must be retained for at least 3 years; employees have a right to inspect and copy their own personnel file annually.
- **Non-competes** are void unless the employee earns at or above the "highly compensated worker" threshold (verify current amount; SB 25-083, Aug 6, 2025) AND the restriction is no broader than necessary to protect trade secrets (CRS § 8-2-113).
- **Biometric data** (fingerprints, iris, voice, face recognition) requires informed, written consent, a written retention/deletion policy, and destruction by the earlier of: original purpose fulfilled, 24 months after last interaction, or 45 days after determining retention is no longer necessary (HB 24-1130, effective July 1, 2025).
- **AI hiring decisions** (Colorado AI Act, SB 24-205, effective 2026-02-01 verify): employers using AI systems for "high-risk" employment decisions must conduct impact assessments, maintain documentation, and notify affected employees. High-risk decisions include hiring, compensation, promotion, and termination.

## 2. Employment Relationship

Colorado is an at-will employment state under common law (no codifying statute; Continental Air Lines v. Keenan, 731 P.2d 708 (Colo. 1987), verify). In the absence of a contract to the contrary, neither employer nor employee is required to give notice or advance notice of termination or resignation, and neither is required to give a reason.

**Separation Notice:** As of May 25, 2022, Colorado employers must provide a written separation notice to all employees whose employment is terminated, regardless of the reason, informing them of unemployment-insurance eligibility and the reason for termination (SB 22-234, amending CRS § 8-90-107(2)). The notice must be delivered at or before the time of separation.

**No mandatory hire notice:** No state rule requires a written offer letter or at-hire notice beyond federal requirements (I-9, tax withholding). Employers should document employment terms to avoid disputes.

**Application Fairness Act:** Effective August 7, 2023, employers cannot request or consider applicant's age, expected graduation date, or other age-related information in hiring decisions (CRS § 8-2-131). Applies to all private employers regardless of size.

## 3. Wages

**Minimum wage:** $15.16 per hour statewide as of January 1, 2026 (CRS § 8-6-102); local jurisdictions (Denver, Boulder, Edgewater) set higher floors. Denver: $19.29/hour (2026). Boulder: verify current rate. Tipped employees must be paid at least $12.14/hour, with tips supplementing to the state minimum.

**Overtime:** Colorado's COMPS Order #40 (7 CCR 1103-1) requires time-and-a-half pay when hours exceed the GREATER OF: (1) 40 hours in a workweek, (2) 12 hours in a workday, OR (3) 12 consecutive hours (regardless of days). When Colorado and federal (FLSA) rules differ, the employee-favorable standard applies. Hours worked in separate workweeks cannot be averaged to avoid overtime. This standard is more protective than federal FLSA overtime rules.

**Pay frequency and pay periods:** Employers shall pay wages at least once per month (CRS § 8-4-102 and § 8-4-103). Pay periods may be monthly, biweekly, weekly, or at termination. When a monthly pay period is used, payment may occur up to 10 business days after the end of the pay period.

**Pay statements:** Employers must provide an itemized pay statement at each wage payment showing: (1) gross pay, (2) each deduction with reason, (3) net pay, (4) pay period dates, and (5) the rate(s) of pay (CRS § 8-4-103(4)). Statements may be electronic and must be provided in a format the employee can understand and retain. Records must be retained for 3 years.

**Final pay on involuntary termination:** Compensation for labor earned, vested, determinable, and unpaid at discharge is due immediately (CRS § 8-4-109). If payroll department is not operational or accessible on the day of discharge, wages are due within 6 hours of the next business day's start. If wages remain unclaimed after 60 days, employer must mail to employee's last-known address. Willful violation can result in treble damages; automatic penalties can double wages owed.

**Final pay on resignation:** Wages for work performed become due on the next regular payday (CRS § 8-4-109). Final paycheck must include all accrued but unused vacation/PTO unless the employment agreement provides otherwise and complies with state law.

**Accrued vacation/PTO:** Must be paid out upon separation (paid or unpaid); "use-it-or-lose-it" policies are void (Colorado Supreme Court, Nieto v. Clark's Market, 2021 CO 27). Employer must pay accrued vacation within 14 days of written demand or face double wages or 10 days' average daily pay, whichever is greater. Vacation constitutes wages under CRS § 8-4-101 et seq.

**Wage deductions and prohibited deductions:** Only legally required deductions (taxes, FICA, unemployment insurance, wage garnishments, court orders) and authorized voluntary deductions (health insurance, 401(k), etc.) are permitted; unlawful deductions make the entire pay subject to wage-claim penalties (CRS § 8-4-105). Employers cannot charge employees for equipment, uniforms, or damage to employer property except where permitted by specific statute or court order. Equipment purchases, uniforms, training costs, and other business-related expenses cannot be deducted from wages or reimbursed through reduced pay. Any violation of deduction rules can result in treble damages and penalties.

**Wage theft penalties:** Colorado law (SB 22-161, as updated by HB 25-1001) imposes strict liability for wage violations. Employees may recover unpaid wages plus penalties, liquidated damages, and attorney fees. Willful violations can result in treble damages (three times unpaid wages). Department of Labor enforcement can pursue civil penalties. Wage claim procedure is a statutory remedy under CRS § 8-4-101 et seq. Recent amendments (HB 25-1001) strengthen enforcement by broadening the definition of wage theft to include minimum wage and overtime violations, restricting mandatory arbitration in wage disputes, and creating expanded liability for employer agents and successors. Violations of final-pay or deduction rules can trigger automatic penalties even absent proof of willfulness.

**Wage claim statute of limitations:** Employees must file wage claims within two years of the unpaid wages becoming due, or within six years for willful violations. Wage claims are filed with the Colorado Department of Labor and Employment, Division of Labor Standards and Statistics, not in court (administrative process, then judicial appeal if necessary). Prevailing employees recover attorney fees and costs.

**Practical wage/hour compliance for HCM Next:** (1) Document all pay changes and overtime calculations. (2) Track hours worked daily, not just aggregated per pay period. (3) Ensure final-pay calculations include all accrued paid leave, commissions, bonuses, and penalties if underpayment occurs. (4) Implement controls to prevent deductions that violate CRS § 8-4-105. (5) Review non-compete agreements for employees earning below highly-compensated threshold; void if unenforceable. (6) Audit job postings for compliance with EPEWA salary-range and benefits requirements. (7) Maintain wage-rate history and selection records for promotions per EPEWA. (8) Flag any wage adjustments or corrections for potential wage-claim exposure if timing suggests retaliatory intent.

## 4. Pay Transparency and Equity

**Equal Pay for Equal Work Act (EPEWA, CRS § 8-5-101 et seq., amended 2024):** Employers shall not pay employees of one sex a wage rate less than the wage rate paid to employees of a different sex for substantially similar work. Applies to all employers with at least one Colorado employee. 2024 amendments require:

- **Pay ranges in postings:** All job postings and notices of job opportunities must include the hourly or salary compensation range for the position.
- **Benefits disclosure:** General description of benefits and other compensation.
- **Application close date:** Anticipated application-window close date.
- **Promotion opportunity notice:** When promoting an employee internally, employers must provide notice to all similarly situated employees before the position is filled, unless the promotion is a lateral transfer without compensation change (CRS § 8-5-102).
- **Post-selection notice:** Within 30 calendar days after the selected candidate begins in the position, employer must make reasonable efforts to notify at minimum the employees with whom the selected candidate works regularly of the hire/promotion.
- **Selection and promotion records:** Employers must maintain job descriptions, wage rate history, and selection documentation for each position for duration of employment plus 3 years.

**Violation penalties:** $500–$10,000 per violation; over 2,800 complaints received as of June 2026 (current enforcement activity verify).

**Salary history ban:** Employers cannot ask about or rely on a prospective employee's salary history to set pay, and cannot discriminate against applicants who refuse to disclose history (CRS § 8-5-103). This applies to compensation, benefits, and other forms of remuneration.

**Pay secrecy ban:** Employers cannot adopt or enforce policies preventing employees from discussing or disclosing their own wages or the wages of coworkers (CRS § 8-5-103); such policies themselves violate EPEWA. Employees have an explicit right to discuss compensation.

**Career progression disclosure:** Employers must provide notice to employees of opportunities for promotion, advancement, and salary/wage progression. Internal job posting requirements apply to all roles except those filled through internal transfer without pay change.

## 5. Leave and Time

**Paid sick leave (Healthy Families and Workplaces Act, CRS § 8-13.3-401 et seq., effective July 15, 2020):** All employers must provide at least 1 hour of accrued paid leave per 30 hours worked, up to 48 hours per year. Expanded as of August 7, 2023 to include bereavement (death of family member, including extended family), weather-related closures, and public health emergency leave. Employees cannot be disciplined, terminated, or have hours reduced for using protected sick leave. Accrual begins on day one of employment. Employer may establish a use-it-or-lose-it deadline of one year per accrual year, but carryover to the next year is required (up to a reasonable cap).

**Paid Family and Medical Leave Insurance (FAMLI, CRS § 8-13.3-501 et seq., effective Jan 1, 2024):** All Colorado employers must provide paid family and medical leave to eligible employees through either the state-run plan or a private plan with equal or greater benefits. Benefits include:

- Up to 12 weeks paid leave for birth, adoption, employee serious illness/medical condition, family member illness/medical condition, bereavement (family member death), or qualifying exigency.
- Additional 4 weeks paid leave for pregnancy-related complications.
- Up to 12 additional weeks for NICU-hospitalized newborns (effective Jan 1, 2026).
- Employee eligibility: $2,500 earned wages in prior 4 calendar quarters; no waiting period.
- Job protection requires 180 days of prior service before leave begins.
- Employer must continue health-insurance contributions during leave.
- Premium rates and caps: Employee/employer contribution split 50/50 (employer can cover fully). State-plan premiums: 0.88% of employee wages up to $184,500 annual cap in 2026; 0.86% in 2027 (verify rates for 2025 and 2028+).
- Coordination with other leave: FAMLI paid leave may run concurrently with unpaid FMLA or other leave policies; employer must apply the most generous standard.

**Voting leave:** Colorado employers must allow employees 2 hours of paid time off during voting hours for a primary or general election (CRS § 1-7-102). Employees should notify employer before election day. Employer may specify when leave is taken if necessary for business operations.

**Jury duty:** Employers cannot discharge, threaten, discipline, or otherwise penalize employees for jury service (CRS § 13-71-126). Employees are entitled to time off for jury duty and cannot lose pay or benefits. Employer may require employee to notify it of jury duty obligation promptly.

**Military leave:** Colorado employers must provide unpaid leave for military service (CRS § 28-3-609). Military members maintain employment rights and cannot be terminated solely due to service. Employer must restore military personnel to comparable positions upon return from service.

**Interaction with pay/role change:** A promotion or base-pay change does not typically affect accrued sick-leave or FAMLI eligibility, but verify whether the new role or compensation level triggers plan changes. Base-pay increase may affect FAMLI benefit calculation if tied to recent wages.

## 6. Records and Access

**Payroll record retention:** Employers must retain records of wage payments, deductions, pay statements, and hours worked for at least 3 years (CRS § 8-4-103). 4 years recommended for tax records. Electronic retention is permitted if all statement information is captured and retrievable.

**Personnel file retention:** Employment agreements, job descriptions, wage-rate history, performance reviews, and disciplinary records should be retained for duration of employment plus 3 years (Colorado Wage Act) to 5 years (recommended for potential Colorado Anti-Discrimination Act (CADA) discrimination claims and statute of limitations). Selection and promotion records must be retained per EPEWA requirements.

**Employee access to personnel file:** Colorado law requires employers to permit employees to inspect and obtain a copy of any part of their own personnel file once per calendar year, without unreasonable delay (CRS § 8-2-129). Employers may charge reasonable costs for copying. Employees cannot access other employees' files or confidential employer information (trade secrets, attorney-client communications). Employer must provide access within a reasonable timeframe (typically 5–10 business days).

**Record format:** No state rule mandating paper vs. electronic, but electronic records must meet the same accuracy, availability, and accessibility standards. Records must be producible in a format the employee can read and retain.

## 7. Privacy and Data

**Colorado Privacy Act (CPA, CRS § 6-1-1301 et seq.):** The CPA does not apply to personal data collected for employment purposes (e.g., payroll, personnel records, job applicant data). Consumers are explicitly excluded if acting in a "commercial or employment context, as a job applicant, or as a beneficiary of someone acting in an employment context." Therefore, most employee data (salary, performance, discipline, benefits) is exempt from CPA access/deletion rights.

**Biometric data exception (HB 24-1130, effective July 1, 2025):** Biometric identifiers (fingerprint, iris, voice, face, hand geometry) and derived biometric data ARE subject to privacy rules even in the employment context. Employers using biometric data must:

- Provide clear, affirmative, informed, written consent before collection.
- Cannot make consent a mandatory condition of employment for routine work unless for enumerated secure purposes (secure-facility access, hardware/software access, identity verification, mobile device unlock, etc.).
- Adopt a written biometric policy with retention schedule, deletion guidelines, and security-incident response protocol.
- Permanently destroy biometric data by the earliest of: original purpose fulfilled, 24 months after last interaction, or 45 days after determining retention is unnecessary.
- Non-compliance incurs damages and injunctive relief.

**Monitoring and surveillance:** No state rule specifically restricting non-biometric electronic monitoring (email, GPS, keystroke logging), but CADA (Colorado Anti-Discrimination Act, CRS § 24-34-402) protects against discrimination based on monitoring results. Employers should document business purposes for monitoring.

**Social media protections:** Employers cannot require employees to disclose personal social media account passwords or access credentials (CRS § 8-2-127). Employer cannot require employee to grant access to personal social media accounts as condition of employment or to discuss or refrain from discussing employment terms on social media.

**Lawful off-duty activities:** Employers cannot discriminate against employees based on lawful conduct conducted off duty and off employer premises, including political activity, smoking/tobacco use, or other legal conduct (CRS § 24-34-402.5). Exception: employer conduct rules apply to behavior that poses substantial risk to employer operations or other employees.

**Breach notification:** Colorado data breach notification law (CRS § 6-1-716) requires employers and other data holders to notify affected individuals of data breaches involving personal information without unreasonable delay or, at latest, 30 days of discovery. Written notice must be provided by mail, email, or telephone. No specific employee-data exemption; general GDPR-adjacent standards apply.

**Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205, effective 2026-02-01 verify):** Employers using artificial intelligence systems to make "high-risk employment decisions" must: (1) conduct impact assessments evaluating potential disparate impact on protected classes; (2) maintain documentation of AI system design, training data, and decision outcomes; (3) notify affected employees when AI is used in hiring, compensation, promotion, or termination decisions; (4) allow employees to request human review of AI decisions affecting them; (5) make impact assessments publicly available or upon request. "High-risk" decisions are those affecting hiring eligibility, compensation, promotion, or termination. Compliance required for systems deployed on or after the effective date. Employers must maintain audit trails of AI-assisted decisions. Colorado Attorney General can enforce violations; private right of action for affected employees may be available. HCM Next must ensure all AI-driven recommendations for promotion or pay changes include impact assessment and employee notification mechanisms.

## 8. Hiring and Background

**Ban the box (Chance to Compete Act, CRS § 8-2-130, effective Sept 1, 2019):** Private employers cannot ask about criminal history on the initial written or electronic application; cannot advertise that they won't hire with criminal records. Background checks may be conducted at any time, but the criminal-history question must be deferred until after initial screening or a conditional job offer. Applies to all employers regardless of size since Sept 1, 2021. Denver and Boulder have stricter rules: conditional job offer required before any background check.

**Application Fairness Act:** Employers cannot request or consider applicant's age or graduation date in hiring decisions (CRS § 8-2-131, effective Aug 7, 2023). Includes any age-proxy information (expected graduation year, class year, etc.). Applies to all private employers. Job applications, background-check requests, and interview questions must avoid age-related inquiries; violation can lead to discrimination claims and penalties.

**FMLA interaction with Colorado law:** Federal Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA, 29 U.S.C. § 2601 et seq.) provides up to 12 weeks unpaid leave for specified reasons and job protection. Colorado FAMLI provides additional paid leave (up to 12 weeks) that may run concurrently or consecutively with FMLA depending on plan design. Employers should coordinate FMLA and FAMLI leave policies to avoid conflicting notices or leave calculations. State law is often more generous; employee gets the benefit of the more protective standard.

**Background check requirements:** No Colorado-specific pre-screening license or training; federal FCRA and Fair Credit Reporting Act rules apply. Employers should follow best practices for adverse action notices.

**Drug testing:** No state rule prohibiting private-employer drug testing; federal requirements and best practices apply.

**E-verify:** No state mandate; federal I-9 and Form I-9 verification apply.

**Salary history inquiry:** Prohibited under EPEWA (see Section 4).

## 9. Separation

**At-will termination:** No advance notice required (common-law at-will; no statute). Employer can terminate for any lawful reason or no reason, provided termination does not violate public policy (wrongful discharge, retaliation for protected activity) or statutory obligation.

**Separation notice:** Must provide written notice including reason for termination and unemployment-insurance eligibility statement (SB 22-234). Notice must be delivered at or before the time of separation. Failure to provide notice can result in penalties and additional claims.

**Final pay timing:** Immediate for involuntary termination (or within 6 hours of next business day if payroll office closed); next regular payday for resignation. Final check must include all accrued vacation/PTO unless forfeiture is permitted by employment agreement and complies with state law.

**Vacation/PTO payout:** Mandatory upon termination. Must pay within 14 days of written demand or face double wages or 10 days' average pay (whichever greater). Vacation constitutes wages under Colorado law.

**WARN Act:** No Colorado mini-WARN act. Federal WARN Act applies (29 U.S.C. § 2101–2109): employers with 100+ employees must give 60 days' written notice of plant closure or mass layoff to affected workers, Colorado Department of Labor and Employment Dislocated Worker Unit, and local government. Penalties: back pay/benefits for each day of insufficient notice + up to $500/day for failure to notify government agencies.

**Non-competes (CRS § 8-2-113, amended Aug 6, 2025):** Generally void. Enforceable only if: (1) employee earns annualized cash compensation at or above the "highly compensated worker" threshold set by state (verify current amount per SB 25-083; formerly ~$75,000–$100,000 range), AND (2) restriction is no broader than necessary to protect legitimate trade secrets. Physician non-competes are categorically void. Sale-of-business covenant is always enforceable. Non-compete must be presented with separate written notice that the employee should consult an attorney.

**Non-solicits:** Colorado law does not explicitly address non-solicitation agreements, but they may be enforceable if narrowly tailored to protect legitimate business interests (trade secrets, customer relationships). Verify enforceability with current case law and CDLE guidance.

## 10. Classification and Multi-State

**Independent contractor test (CRS § 8-6-101 et seq.):** Colorado uses a modified ABC test requiring BOTH:

- **(A) Control:** Worker is free from control and direction in the performance of service.
- **(C) Business relationship:** Worker is customarily engaged in an independent trade, occupation, profession, or business related to the service performed.

(Note: Colorado does not require the standard ABC **(B)** prong—that the work be outside the hiring entity's core business.) Burden of proof is on the employer. A properly drafted written contract is necessary but insufficient. Nine statutory factors indicate contractor status; employer must demonstrate majority are present. Misclassification can result in back wages, penalties, and unemployment insurance liability.

**Remote worker / multi-state:** If a Colorado employee works remotely in another state, Colorado law applies to the at-hire relationship and wage/overtime rules unless the employee's primary work location is in a state with stricter standards (verify conflict-of-laws guidance). Payroll records must comply with the employee's actual workstate. Tax withholding must comply with the state where work is performed. Minimum wage of the higher state applies (e.g., Colorado resident working remotely in California gets California minimum). Overtime rules apply from the state where the work is primarily performed; if split between states, apply the most protective standard.

**Out-of-state employer hiring Colorado worker:** If the employer lacks Colorado presence but hires a Colorado resident, Colorado wage, anti-discrimination (CADA), and FAMLI laws apply to that employee. Employer may be subject to Colorado state income tax reporting and unemployment insurance contributions for that employee. Colorado wage and hour laws apply regardless of whether the work is performed in Colorado or remotely elsewhere.

**Nine statutory factors for contractor status:** Colorado law enumerates nine factors to evaluate contractor status under CRS § 8-6-101. The employer must prove the majority of factors indicate contractor status. These factors include: (1) worker's ability to hire/fire others and control subcontractors, (2) worker's right to terminate the relationship at will, (3) relationship as permanent/indefinite vs. project-based, (4) whether work is integral to employer's business or peripheral, (5) nature of work relationship (personal services, delegation rights), (6) whether work is typically performed by independent contractors in the industry, (7) place of work (independent vs. employer's premises), (8) control of premises and tools/equipment, and (9) provision of materials and tools by the worker. Misclassification as contractor when employee status was warranted results in employer liability for back wages (with prejudgment interest), unpaid overtime, FAMLI/paid sick leave benefits contributions, unemployment insurance premiums, workers' compensation insurance liability, payroll taxes, penalties, and possible treble damages. Department of Labor can initiate classification audits and wage investigations. Reclassification can trigger multi-year audit exposure.

### Anti-discrimination and retaliation (CADA, C.R.S. § 24-34-402; supplement to section 2)

**Colorado Anti-Discrimination Act (CADA, CRS § 24-34-402 et seq.):** Colorado prohibits employment discrimination based on protected classifications: race, color, religion, national origin, ancestry, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression, marital status, parental status, disability, age (40+), genetic information, or lawful off-duty conduct. Applies to all employers with one or more Colorado employees. Discrimination in hiring, compensation, job placement, promotion, termination, and all terms/conditions of employment is unlawful.

**CADA enforcement and remedies:** Complainants file charges with the Colorado Civil Rights Division (CCRD) within 180 days of the discriminatory act (or up to one year if CCRD has ongoing negotiations). CCRD investigates and may attempt conciliation. If no resolution, complainant can seek a "right to sue" letter and file a private lawsuit for back pay, front pay, compensatory damages (including emotional distress), punitive damages, attorney fees, and costs. Employers with ongoing violations can face civil penalties and injunctive relief.

**Anti-retaliation and anti-harassment:** Retaliation against employees for complaining about discrimination, filing CCRD charges, or participating in CCRD proceedings is unlawful. Retaliation includes discharge, demotion, pay reduction, schedule changes, or any adverse employment action taken because of protected activity. Hostile-work-environment harassment—unwelcome conduct based on protected characteristics that is severe or pervasive enough to alter the terms/conditions of employment—violates CADA. Employers have affirmative duty to maintain workplaces free of harassment and to investigate complaints promptly. Recent amendment (POWR Act 2023) clarifies that NDAs, severance agreements, and settlement agreements cannot silence reporting of unlawful workplace conduct. Confidentiality provisions cannot restrict disclosure to government agencies, union representatives, attorneys, or in legal proceedings. Agreements attempting to do so are void.

**Record retention for CADA claims:** Employers must retain all personnel records, complaint files, investigation documents, and disciplinary records for minimum 5 years to support defense against discrimination claims.

**Retaliation and wage-related claims:** Colorado law protects employees who report wage violations, file wage claims, complain about wage/hour issues, or participate in wage-related litigation. Employers cannot discharge, demote, suspend, or otherwise retaliate against employees exercising these rights. Retaliation claims can run concurrently with wage claims, potentially tripling liability if retaliation is proven. Filing a wage claim itself is protected activity; adverse employment action taken after filing creates a retaliation claim.

## 11. Implications for P1A/P1B

**P1A (Promotion):**

- Model job-posting and selection-notice obligations as a timed requirement: posting must include salary range and close date; selection notice must be sent within 30 calendar days (CRS § 8-5-102).
- Before posting, notify similarly situated employees of the opportunity unless it is a lateral transfer without compensation change.
- Record the promotion as part of wage-rate history for 3+ years; store job description and selection documentation.
- If promoted to manager or into a new pay band, trigger a new pay-statement cycle and verify no salary-history reliance occurred.
- Model non-compete enforceability check: is the promotee above the highly-compensated threshold? If not, non-competes are void.
- Verify whether manager change triggers posting/notice or is a lateral reassignment.

**P1B (Base-Pay Change):**

- Prohibit using employee's prior salary to set new pay; enforce pay-secrecy ban on employer policies (CRS § 8-5-103).
- All pay changes must be documented in a new pay statement; wages must include the new rate on the next regular payday or within the pay frequency (CRS § 8-4-102). Pay statements must show the new rate and effective date.
- If raising pay, verify no equal-pay violation for other employees in similar roles (CRS § 8-5-101). Conduct pay-equity analysis to ensure no employee of different protected class is paid less for substantially similar work.
- Model pay-change recording to support EPEWA equal-pay compliance (wage-rate history, position, gender of all similarly situated workers). Retain documentation for 3+ years.
- Termination or layoff scenario: verify final pay (immediate if involuntary, or within 6 hours of next business day if payroll closed) and vacation payout (14-day deadline after written demand) are calculated at the most recent rate. If employer disputes accrued vacation amount, document the accrual methodology.
- If base-pay change also triggers a promotion, follow both P1A and P1B workflows concurrently. Job posting requirements apply; selection notice required within 30 days.
- Pay-increase notifications: ensure no pay-secrecy policy prevents employees from discussing their new compensation. Written offer or documentation recommended to avoid disputes.

**Open questions:**

- Verify the current "highly compensated worker" threshold for non-compete enforceability (SB 25-083 effective Aug 6, 2025; likely $75,000–$127,091 range but confirm exact amount). Threshold may adjust annually for inflation.
- Confirm whether manager lateral reassignment (same pay, same level) requires posting/notice under EPEWA or if "job opportunity" is limited to promotion, demotion, or pay change. Internal posting rules may depend on salary band changes.
- Clarify FAMLI interaction with unpaid leave (e.g., can FAMLI paid leave run concurrently with unpaid FMLA leave? Can employee stack paid + unpaid leave?).
- Confirm whether severance must be included in final pay or can be a separate disbursement post-termination. Statutory final-pay rules may not require severance in final check if lawfully deferred.
- Verify COMPS Order #40 overtime calculation rules for shift changes (e.g., does a mid-week role change reset the 12-hour threshold? How are split shifts treated?).
- Verify applicability of AI Act requirements to HCM Next and when notification to employees is required. Clarify which systems qualify as "high-risk" employment decisions.
- Confirm Boulder and Denver local minimum-wage rules and applicability to remote workers based elsewhere.
- Verify whether job-posting salary ranges must be disclosed publicly or only on request, and whether ranges must cover all compensation or base salary only.
- Verify whether HCM Next's internal promotion workflows must include notice to all employees before posting (current language requires "reasonable efforts").
- Confirm whether accrued vacation is required to be paid out on voluntary resignation or only on involuntary termination.
